# NCUA Exam Preparation: The Complete Guide for Credit Union Leaders

Your NCUA exam is not a pop quiz. It is scheduled, predictable, and -- if you run your credit union with daily discipline -- it should be the easiest week of your year.

But that is not how most credit union leaders experience it. In 25 years of working with credit unions, I have watched exam season turn competent executive teams into scrambling, stressed-out document hunters. Three weeks before the examiner arrives, the CFO is pulling all-nighters assembling binders. The compliance officer is chasing down board minutes from six months ago. The CEO is rehearsing talking points instead of running the credit union.

It does not have to work that way. The credit unions that walk into exams with confidence are not the ones with the best cramming strategy. They are the ones that built a year-round discipline of documented decisions, daily financial awareness, and clear accountability. When the examiner shows up, they hand over the documentation and get back to work.

This guide covers the entire NCUA examination lifecycle -- from pre-exam preparation to post-exam follow-up -- written from the perspective of someone who has been on the credit union side of the table for over two decades. It is not a compliance checklist you file and forget. It is a framework for making exam readiness a permanent part of how you run your institution.

## What an NCUA Exam Actually Looks Like

If you have never been through an NCUA examination -- or if it has been a while -- here is what to expect.

### Frequency and Scheduling

Most federally insured credit unions are examined annually. Some smaller or lower-risk credit unions may be on an 18-month cycle. Higher-risk institutions or those with prior findings may be examined more frequently. The NCUA uses a risk-based scheduling approach, which means credit unions with complex balance sheets, rapid growth, recent management changes, or prior exam issues are more likely to see examiners sooner.

You will receive advance notice before the examination, typically 30 to 60 days. The Examiner-in-Charge (EIC) will send a pre-exam document request through the MERIT system -- the NCUA's online examination management portal. This document request is your starting gun.

### The MERIT System

MERIT (Modern Examination and Risk Identification Tool) is the NCUA's portal for managing the examination process. You will use it to upload requested documents, communicate with the examination team, and receive your final report. If your team has not used MERIT before, log in well ahead of the exam to familiarize yourselves with the interface. There is nothing worse than wasting examiner time because your team cannot figure out how to upload documents.

### Examination Phases

A typical NCUA exam follows this flow:

1. **Pre-exam planning (30-60 days before).** The EIC reviews your previous exam report, call report data, off-site monitoring results, and any interim contacts. They identify risk areas to focus on and send the document request.

2. **Document submission (2-4 weeks before).** Your team uploads the requested documents through MERIT. The more complete and organized your submission, the smoother the on-site portion will go.

3. **On-site examination (1-2 weeks).** The examination team -- typically two to four examiners for a mid-size credit union -- arrives and begins their review. The shift toward hybrid examinations means some of this work may happen remotely, but expect significant on-site presence. The EIC will hold an entrance conference with your management team to outline the scope and timeline.

4. **Fieldwork and review.** Examiners dig into your financials, policies, minutes, loan files, compliance programs, and IT systems. They will ask questions -- lots of them. They will request additional documents. They will want to interview key staff.

5. **Exit conference.** The EIC presents preliminary findings to management and the board. This is not the final report, but it gives you a clear picture of where you stand. Listen carefully, take notes, and ask clarifying questions. Do not argue.

6. **Final report.** The examination report is delivered through MERIT, typically within 60 days of the exit conference. It includes your CAMELS composite and component ratings, examination findings, and any required corrective actions.

### Timeline

For a credit union in the $100M to $2B range, expect the on-site portion to last one to two weeks. The total process from document request to final report is typically 90 to 120 days.

## The CAMELS Rating System Explained

Your CAMELS rating is the outcome of the examination. It is a composite score from 1 (best) to 5 (worst), derived from six component ratings. Understanding what examiners evaluate in each component helps you focus your preparation where it matters most.

### C -- Capital Adequacy

Examiners assess whether your credit union has enough capital to absorb losses and support growth. They look at your net worth ratio (the credit union equivalent of a capital ratio), its trend over time, concentration risk, and your capital planning process. For most well-managed credit unions in the $100M-$2B range, capital adequacy is not the primary concern -- but deteriorating trends or thin capital cushions will draw attention.

**What a "1" looks like:** Net worth ratio well above 7% (well-capitalized threshold), positive trends, strong capital planning, no concentrations threatening capital.

**What a "3" looks like:** Net worth ratio adequate but trending down, capital planning is informal, concentrations are identified but not well-managed.

**What a "5" looks like:** Net worth ratio approaching or below adequately capitalized threshold, capital plan is inadequate, losses are eroding capital with no clear remediation path.

### A -- Asset Quality

This is where examiners spend significant time, especially in 2026. They evaluate your loan portfolio quality, delinquency trends, charge-off rates, Allowance for Credit Losses (ACL) methodology and adequacy, concentration risk, and investment portfolio risk. With the NCUA's 2026 supervisory priorities explicitly calling out loan quality, delinquency, and charge-offs, expect deep scrutiny here.

**Key metrics examiners track:** 60-day+ delinquency ratio, net charge-off ratio, ACL coverage ratio, largest loan concentrations as a percentage of capital, troubled debt restructurings.

### M -- Management

This component is the most subjective -- and often the most consequential. Examiners evaluate the quality of your leadership, strategic planning, risk management frameworks, internal controls, audit program, and governance practices. They review board minutes, management reports, policy adherence, and how well your team can articulate the credit union's risk position and strategic direction.

A strong Management rating requires evidence that your leadership team is actively managing the institution, not just monitoring it. Examiners look for decisions -- documented decisions with rationale, assigned owners, timelines, and follow-up. If your board minutes read like a rubber-stamp exercise, that tells the examiner something about management quality that no financial ratio can offset.

### E -- Earnings

Are you making enough money to sustain operations, build capital, and serve members? Examiners evaluate net interest margin, non-interest income and expense trends, operating efficiency, and return on assets relative to peers. They also look at earnings quality -- are your earnings sustainable, or are they propped up by one-time gains or unsustainable pricing strategies?

A $500M credit union I worked with had a solid ROA of 0.85% -- until the examiner pointed out that 22 basis points of it came from non-recurring investment gains. The sustainable ROA was closer to 0.63%, which changed the entire conversation about capital planning and growth capacity.

### L -- Liquidity

Can you meet your obligations? Examiners assess your on-balance-sheet liquidity, borrowing capacity and utilization, deposit stability, contingency funding plan, and cash flow projections. They want to see that you understand where your cash comes from, where it goes, and what happens if the pattern changes.

Credit unions over $50M are required to maintain a board-approved contingency funding plan under NCUA regulation 741.12. If you do not have one, that is a finding. If you have one but your team cannot walk the examiner through it, that is also a finding.

For a deeper look at how ALM connects to liquidity and interest rate risk exam readiness, see our [asset liability management guide for credit unions](/resources/asset-liability-management-credit-unions).

### S -- Sensitivity to Market Risk

Added to the CAMELS framework in the 2022 supervisory update, the Sensitivity component evaluates your credit union's exposure to changes in interest rates, exchange rates (rarely applicable to credit unions), and other market risk factors. Examiners review your interest rate risk modeling, assumptions, stress testing, ALCO governance, and balance sheet positioning.

This is the component where ALM preparation directly meets exam readiness. If your ALM program is sound -- models validated, assumptions current, ALCO actively managing risk, documentation complete -- the "S" rating takes care of itself. If your ALM program is a quarterly compliance exercise with stale assumptions, this is where you will get flagged.

For detailed guidance on building an IRR framework that satisfies examiner expectations, read our guide to [interest rate risk management for credit unions](/resources/interest-rate-risk-management-credit-unions).

## NCUA 2026 Supervisory Priorities

Every year, the NCUA publishes a supervisory priorities letter that tells you exactly what examiners will focus on. This is not a secret -- it is published guidance that tells you where to prepare. The 2026 priorities center on five areas:

### 1. Balance Sheet Management and Lending

This is the top priority for 2026. Examiners will scrutinize:
- **Loan quality and delinquency trends.** After years of historically low credit losses, delinquency rates have climbed. Examiners will evaluate whether your credit quality metrics are trending in the right direction and whether your management team is responding appropriately.
- **Charge-off levels and ACL adequacy.** Is your Allowance for Credit Losses keeping pace with actual loss experience? Has your ACL methodology been updated to reflect current conditions, or are you still using assumptions from the low-loss years?
- **Concentration risk.** Large concentrations in any single loan type, industry, or geography will receive close examination. Member business lending concentrations above regulatory thresholds are a particular focus.

### 2. Interest Rate and Liquidity Risk

The prolonged period of elevated rates has stressed credit union balance sheets. Examiners will assess:
- Interest rate risk modeling and the reasonableness of assumptions
- Deposit stability and repricing behavior
- Reliance on wholesale or non-member funding
- Liquidity position and contingency planning

### 3. Operational Risk

This category covers:
- **Fraud prevention** -- particularly ACH and wire transfer fraud, which has surged
- **Payment system risk** -- including real-time payment exposure and third-party processor oversight
- **Cybersecurity** -- incident response capabilities, vulnerability management, and IT governance

### 4. Compliance Risk

BSA/AML compliance remains a perennial focus. Examiners also evaluate consumer protection compliance, fair lending practices, and regulatory reporting accuracy.

### 5. The "No Regulation by Enforcement" Approach

The NCUA has signaled a shift toward working collaboratively with credit unions rather than relying on enforcement actions for first-time issues. This does not mean lower standards -- it means examiners will provide more guidance and corrective direction before escalating to formal enforcement. For credit unions that respond promptly to examiner concerns, this is a positive development.

For a detailed analysis of each 2026 priority and specific preparation strategies, see our [NCUA 2026 supervisory priorities breakdown](/resources/ncua-supervisory-priorities-2026).

## Pre-Exam Preparation: Your 90-Day Checklist

Do not start preparing when the document request arrives. Start 90 days before your expected exam date. If you do not know your expected date, assume it is 12 months after your last exam and work backward.

### 90 Days Out: Self-Assessment

- **Review your prior exam report.** Pull out every finding, recommendation, and Document of Resolution. For each one, ask: did we address this? Can we prove it? Where is the documentation?
- **Run a self-assessment against CAMELS components.** Rate yourself honestly on each component. Where are you weakest? That is where to focus your preparation.
- **Update all policies.** Review your IRR policy, liquidity policy, BSA/AML policy, lending policy, investment policy, and information security policy. If any policy has not been updated in the past 12 months, update it now. Stale policies are one of the most common exam findings.
- **Verify board minutes are complete.** Every board meeting for the past 12 months should have minutes that document attendance, discussion, and decisions. If there are gaps, address them now.

### 60 Days Out: Document Assembly

- **Create your exam preparation folder.** Organize all documents the examiner will need by CAMELS component. Make it easy for someone who has never seen your credit union to find what they need.
- **Update your ACL analysis.** Make sure your Allowance for Credit Losses reflects current loss experience and economic conditions. Document your methodology and assumptions.
- **Review your ALM model.** Confirm your model assumptions are current, your most recent model run is complete, and your validation is up to date.
- **Prepare your management reports.** Assemble the last 12 months of board reports, ALCO minutes, management financial reports, and any special committee reports.
- **Test your contingency funding plan.** Can your team walk through it? Do the funding sources and dollar amounts reflect current relationships and limits?

### 30 Days Out: Final Preparation

- **Designate your exam point of contact.** This person will be the primary liaison with the examination team. Typically the CFO, VP of Finance, or compliance officer. This person needs the authority and time to be responsive.
- **Brief your team.** Everyone who may interact with examiners should understand the process, know their role, and be prepared to answer questions about their area of responsibility. Do not script them -- examiners can tell when answers are rehearsed. But make sure they know what to expect.
- **Set up your MERIT access.** Verify that your exam point of contact and key staff have active MERIT accounts and know how to upload documents.
- **Review your strategic plan.** Examiners will ask about your strategic direction. Make sure your leadership team can articulate the plan, progress against goals, and how you are tracking execution.
- **Clean up your exception tracking.** Policy exceptions, audit findings, and compliance items should all show clear documentation of the exception, approval authority, and disposition.

### When the Document Request Arrives

Respond completely and promptly. Every day of delay in providing documents extends the on-site portion of the exam and signals to the examiner that your documentation practices may be disorganized. Aim to upload everything within five business days of receiving the request.

If you cannot provide a requested document because it does not exist, say so directly. Do not create documents retroactively to fill gaps. Examiners are experienced enough to spot documents that were created to satisfy a request rather than as part of normal operations. It is far better to acknowledge a gap and explain your plan to address it than to fabricate evidence of a practice that does not exist.

## What Examiners Actually Review

Understanding what examiners look at helps you prepare the right materials and anticipate their questions. Here is a walkthrough of the major review areas:

### Loan Underwriting and Credit Administration

Examiners will sample your loan portfolio -- typically 20 to 50 individual loans depending on portfolio size and complexity. They evaluate:
- Underwriting quality against your own policies
- Credit analysis documentation
- Collateral valuation and documentation
- Exception tracking and approval authority
- Collection practices and workout documentation for troubled loans

A $300M credit union I worked with had solid underwriting standards on paper -- but the examiner found that 18% of sampled loans had documentation exceptions that were not tracked or approved through the proper channels. The loans themselves were performing, but the documentation weakness led to a Management component downgrade. The credit union's actual credit decisions were sound. Their paperwork was not. That distinction mattered.

### Investment Portfolio

Examiners review your investment holdings for credit risk, interest rate risk, concentration risk, and compliance with your investment policy. They want to see:
- Investment policy that reflects NCUA regulations (Part 703)
- Due diligence documentation for each investment type
- Portfolio monitoring reports showing risk metrics
- Evidence that the board reviews and approves the investment strategy

### Earnings Analysis

Beyond the headline ROA and net interest margin, examiners dig into:
- Trends over the past eight quarters
- Comparison to peer group performance
- Non-interest income composition and sustainability
- Operating expense trends and efficiency ratios
- Budget versus actual variance analysis

### BSA/AML Program

Bank Secrecy Act compliance is always a high-priority review area. Examiners evaluate:
- Your BSA risk assessment -- is it current and does it reflect your actual membership and product mix?
- Currency Transaction Report (CTR) filing accuracy and timeliness
- Suspicious Activity Report (SAR) filing practices
- Customer Due Diligence (CDD) and beneficial ownership compliance
- Training program for staff
- Independent testing results

BSA findings can escalate quickly. A single pattern of unreported suspicious activity can result in enforcement action. This is one area where "good enough" is not good enough.

### Cybersecurity and IT Governance

Examiners assess your information security program against NCUA's expectations and FFIEC guidance:
- Information security risk assessment
- Incident response plan (and evidence of testing)
- Access controls and authentication practices
- Vendor management for technology service providers
- Business continuity and disaster recovery planning

### Board Minutes and Strategic Planning

This is where the Management component rating often lives or dies. Examiners read your board minutes looking for evidence of:
- Active discussion (not just rubber-stamping management recommendations)
- Understanding of risk position
- Deliberate decision-making with documented rationale
- Follow-up on prior decisions and audit findings
- Strategic plan monitoring and progress tracking

## The Documentation Advantage

This is the section that separates credit unions that dread exams from credit unions that welcome them.

Examiners review evidence, not intentions. You can have the best risk management practices in the industry, but if you cannot document them, they do not exist in the examiner's eyes. Conversely, a credit union with average performance but excellent documentation will consistently receive better ratings than a better-performing institution with poor records.

### What Year-Round Documentation Looks Like

The credit unions that walk into exams with confidence have built documentation into their daily operating rhythm:

- **Decision records.** Every significant decision -- pricing changes, policy exceptions, staffing decisions, vendor selections, product changes -- is documented with the rationale, the data that informed it, who made it, and the expected outcome.

- **Financial trail.** The daily bottom line -- the spread between money bought and money sold -- is tracked and visible. When the examiner asks "what happened to your margin in October?" you do not wait for accounting to pull reports. You pull up the daily record and show them exactly what happened, when you saw it, and what you did about it.

- **Meeting minutes that show work.** Board minutes, ALCO minutes, and committee minutes document real discussion, real analysis, and real decisions. Not just "the board reviewed the financial report and approved." But "the board discussed the 12 basis point margin compression observed over the past 30 days, reviewed management's analysis attributing it to a 23% increase in cost of funds driven by CD repricing, and approved management's recommendation to adjust money market pricing by 15 basis points effective November 1."

- **Exception tracking.** Every policy exception is logged with the exception details, approval authority, risk assessment, and follow-up requirements.

- **Audit follow-up.** Internal and external audit findings are tracked with corrective actions, responsible parties, deadlines, and completion evidence.

### The Cost of Poor Documentation

A $600M credit union I worked with had fundamentally sound financial management. Their capital was strong, asset quality was solid, earnings were above peer. But their exam resulted in a Management component rating of "3" -- below their expectations and below what their actual performance warranted. The reason: examiners found incomplete board minutes for three months, no documented rationale for a major pricing decision, and ALCO minutes that consisted of a single paragraph per meeting.

The credit union was managing well. They just could not prove it. After implementing a daily documentation discipline -- tracking the bottom line every day, documenting every decision in two-week loops, and building a real audit trail -- their next exam resulted in a Management "2" with examiner comments specifically noting the improvement in documentation practices.

## Common Exam Findings and How to Avoid Them

After 25 years of working with credit unions through exam cycles, these are the findings I see most often -- and the practical fixes for each.

### 1. Inadequate ACL Methodology

**The finding:** Your Allowance for Credit Losses does not reflect current conditions or lacks documentation supporting the methodology and assumptions used.

**The fix:** Review and update your ACL methodology at least quarterly. Document the qualitative factors you consider, the data sources you use, and the rationale for any adjustments. If you are still using a pre-CECL framework, update immediately -- CECL has been required for credit unions since 2023.

### 2. Stale Policies

**The finding:** Policies have not been reviewed or updated within the past 12 months, or policy language does not reflect current regulatory requirements or credit union practices.

**The fix:** Implement an annual policy review calendar. Assign each policy to a responsible officer with a review date. When policies are reviewed, document what changed and why -- even if the answer is "no changes needed." Examiners want to see evidence of review, not just current dates on cover pages.

### 3. Insufficient IRR Documentation

**The finding:** Interest rate risk modeling lacks documentation of assumptions, or assumptions have not been updated to reflect current conditions. Model validation is overdue.

**The fix:** Document every material assumption in your ALM model -- deposit decay rates, prepayment speeds, pricing betas, new business volume projections. Review and update these assumptions at least annually, or more frequently if conditions change materially. Schedule model validation on a three-year cycle and stick to it.

### 4. Weak BSA Risk Assessment

**The finding:** Your BSA risk assessment does not reflect your current membership composition, product mix, or geographic risk factors.

**The fix:** Update your BSA risk assessment annually. Make sure it covers all products and services (including newer offerings like online account opening, P2P payments, and cryptocurrency-related activity if applicable). Document the methodology and data sources used.

### 5. Missing or Incomplete Board Minutes

**The finding:** Board minutes do not adequately document discussion, deliberation, and decision-making. Minutes are missing for one or more meetings.

**The fix:** This is a discipline problem, not a skills problem. Assign a dedicated board secretary (not the CEO) to take minutes. Use a consistent template that captures attendance, agenda items, discussion summaries, motions, votes, and action items. Review and approve minutes at the following meeting. Never skip this step.

### 6. Lack of Vendor Management Oversight

**The finding:** The credit union does not have a comprehensive vendor management program, or due diligence documentation for critical vendors is missing or incomplete.

**The fix:** Inventory all vendors and categorize by risk (critical, significant, routine). For critical vendors -- core processing, online banking, card processing, ALM modeling -- maintain due diligence files including financial reviews, SOC reports, BCP testing, and contract terms. Review critical vendor relationships at least annually and document the review.

## Building a Year-Round Exam-Ready Culture

The exam is one week. The preparation is 52 weeks. If you are scrambling in the 30 days before an exam, you have a systems problem, not a preparation problem.

Building an exam-ready culture means integrating three disciplines into your daily operations:

### 1. Daily Financial Awareness

You should know your bottom line every day. Not the month-end close. Not the quarterly ALCO report. Every day. When you know the spread between money bought and money sold on a daily basis, two things happen: you catch problems weeks earlier than monthly reporting allows, and you build a financial documentation trail that examiners can follow.

A $350M credit union that implemented daily bottom-line tracking walked into their next exam with 365 days of daily financial records. The examiner spent 30 minutes reviewing the financial trail instead of three days reconstructing it from monthly reports. That alone shortened the on-site exam by a day.

### 2. Documented Decisions

Every decision should have a record. Not because the examiner wants it -- although they do -- but because you cannot improve what you do not track. A documented decision includes:
- What was decided
- What data informed the decision
- Who made it
- What the expected outcome was
- When you will check whether it worked

This discipline, practiced in tight two-week loops, produces an audit trail that examiners recognize immediately as evidence of active management.

### 3. Accountability Loops

A decision without follow-up is just a good intention. Your operating rhythm should include regular check-ins on prior decisions: did the pricing change produce the expected result? Did the marketing campaign hit the deposit growth target? Did the staffing adjustment improve per-person productivity?

When examiners see this kind of closed-loop management -- decide, execute, measure, adjust -- it tells them more about your management quality than any financial ratio.

### The Mindset Shift

Stop thinking about exam preparation as a project with a start and end date. Start thinking about it as your operating system. The credit unions that receive consistently strong CAMELS ratings are not the ones that prepare the hardest before each exam. They are the ones that run their institution every day the way they want the examiner to see it.

## During the Exam: How to Work With Your Examiner

Your examiner is not your adversary. They are a regulator doing important work to protect your members' deposits and the credit union system. How you work with them affects the tone and outcome of the examination.

### Practical Tips

- **Designate a single point of contact.** All examiner requests should flow through one person who can coordinate responses across departments. This prevents conflicting information and ensures nothing falls through the cracks.

- **Provide complete, organized documents.** When examiners request additional materials during the on-site exam, respond within the same business day. Organize documents logically with clear labels. The easier you make the examiner's job, the smoother the exam goes.

- **Be responsive, not defensive.** When an examiner asks a question, answer it directly. If you do not know, say "I do not know, but I will get you that information by end of day." Do not speculate, do not deflect, and do not offer unsolicited explanations.

- **Do not argue with preliminary findings.** At the exit conference, listen carefully to the EIC's preliminary findings. Ask clarifying questions: "Can you help me understand the specific concern?" or "What documentation would you need to see to address this?" But do not argue. If you disagree with a finding, the proper channel is the formal response process after the report is issued.

- **Take detailed notes.** Assign someone to take notes during every substantive conversation with examiners. These notes become your roadmap for addressing any findings and preparing for the next exam.

- **Keep your team calm.** Your staff will take their cues from you. If leadership treats the exam as routine and manageable, the team follows suit. If leadership panics, the team panics -- and panicked employees give disorganized answers that create more examiner questions.

## After the Exam: Responding to Findings

The exam does not end at the exit conference. How you respond to findings determines whether issues compound or resolve.

### Reading the Report

When you receive the final examination report through MERIT:
- Read the entire report carefully, including the confidential section if applicable.
- Note your composite and component CAMELS ratings.
- Identify all findings, recommendations, and any Documents of Resolution (DOR).
- Distinguish between findings (issues that need corrective action) and recommendations (suggestions for improvement). Both matter, but findings carry more urgency.

### Prioritizing Corrective Actions

Not all findings are equal. Prioritize based on:
1. **Safety and soundness concerns** -- anything affecting capital, liquidity, or solvency comes first.
2. **Repeat findings** -- issues flagged in prior exams that were not fully resolved. These carry more weight and can escalate to enforcement action.
3. **DOR requirements** -- Documents of Resolution have specific deadlines and reporting requirements. These are non-negotiable.
4. **Compliance issues** -- BSA, fair lending, and consumer protection findings require prompt corrective action.
5. **Operational recommendations** -- important but typically lower urgency.

### Documenting Your Response

For each finding, document:
- Your understanding of the finding and its root cause
- The specific corrective action you will take
- The person responsible for implementation
- The timeline for completion
- How you will verify the corrective action was effective

### Closing the Loop

Track every corrective action to completion. When the next exam cycle begins, your examiner will review your response to prior findings as one of the first things they do. Incomplete corrective actions are a red flag that tells the examiner your management team does not follow through.

A discipline of documented decisions and accountability loops does not just help you during the exam -- it is how you close findings decisively and demonstrate to the next examiner that your management team takes regulatory feedback seriously.

## FAQ: NCUA Exam Preparation

### How do I prepare for an NCUA exam?

Start 90 days before your expected exam date. Review your prior exam findings and verify all corrective actions are complete. Update all policies to reflect current conditions and regulatory requirements. Assemble documentation organized by CAMELS component. Run a self-assessment against each CAMELS area to identify weaknesses. Brief your team on the examination process and their roles. Verify MERIT access for your exam point of contact. Most importantly, build daily documentation practices year-round so that exam preparation is a matter of organizing existing records rather than creating them from scratch.

### What does the NCUA look for in an examination?

NCUA examiners evaluate your credit union across six CAMELS components: Capital adequacy, Asset quality, Management, Earnings, Liquidity, and Sensitivity to market risk. Within each component, they review financial metrics, policies and procedures, board and management governance, documentation, and evidence of active risk management. For 2026, specific focus areas include loan quality and delinquency trends, ACL adequacy, interest rate and liquidity risk management, cybersecurity, and BSA/AML compliance. Examiners are looking for evidence that your management team understands the credit union's risk position and is actively managing it through documented decisions and follow-through.

### How often does the NCUA examine credit unions?

Most federally insured credit unions are examined annually. Some smaller credit unions with consistently strong CAMELS ratings may be on an 18-month examination cycle. Credit unions with elevated risk profiles -- due to rapid growth, management changes, financial deterioration, or prior exam findings -- may be examined more frequently. The NCUA uses a risk-based scheduling approach that considers the credit union's asset size, complexity, financial condition, and prior examination results to determine the appropriate examination frequency.

### What is the CAMELS rating system?

CAMELS is the rating system used by the NCUA to evaluate credit union safety and soundness. It consists of six components: Capital adequacy (C), Asset quality (A), Management (M), Earnings (E), Liquidity (L), and Sensitivity to market risk (S). Each component is rated from 1 (strongest) to 5 (weakest), and a composite rating is assigned based on the overall assessment. A composite rating of 1 or 2 indicates a fundamentally sound institution. A rating of 3 indicates some degree of supervisory concern. Ratings of 4 or 5 indicate significant or critical concerns requiring immediate corrective action.

### How long does an NCUA exam take?

For credit unions in the $100M to $2B asset range, the on-site examination typically lasts one to two weeks. The total examination process -- from the initial document request through the final report -- spans approximately 90 to 120 days. The on-site duration depends on the credit union's complexity, the number of risk areas requiring detailed review, the completeness and organization of pre-submitted documents, and any issues that arise during fieldwork. Credit unions that submit complete, well-organized documents through MERIT before the on-site visit can significantly reduce the time examiners spend on-site.
