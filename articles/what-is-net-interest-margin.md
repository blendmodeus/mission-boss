# What Is Net Interest Margin? A Plain-Language Guide for Credit Union Leaders

If you run a credit union and you had to pick one number that tells you whether your financial engine is running or stalling, net interest margin is that number. Not ROA. Not the efficiency ratio. Not loan growth. Those matter, but they are downstream. Net interest margin is the source -- the spread between the rate you bought money at and the rate you sold it for. Everything else flows from it.

The problem is that most credit union leaders only see their NIM once a month, sometimes less. By the time the number lands on the board packet, it is 30 to 45 days old. You are steering with a rearview mirror bolted to a six-week delay. This article will give you a clear understanding of what net interest margin is, how it works, what the current benchmarks look like, and why the frequency at which you monitor it might matter more than the number itself.

## Net Interest Margin Defined

Net interest margin (NIM) is the difference between the interest income a credit union earns on its loans and investments and the interest expense it pays on deposits and borrowings, expressed as a percentage of average earning assets.

In plain language: it is the spread between money bought and money sold.

**The formula:**

> NIM = (Interest Income - Interest Expense) / Average Earning Assets

Here is what that looks like with real numbers. Take a $350M credit union:

- **Interest income** (loans, investments, fed funds): $18.2M annually
- **Interest expense** (share dividends, CDs, borrowings): $8.4M annually
- **Net interest income**: $9.8M
- **Average earning assets**: $310M

> NIM = $9.8M / $310M = **3.16%**

That 3.16% is the margin the credit union has to cover operating expenses, build reserves, and serve its members. When it compresses, everything tightens. When it expands, you have room to invest, hire, and grow.

## Net Interest Margin vs. Net Interest Income

These two terms get confused constantly, so let's clear it up.

**Net interest income (NII)** is the dollar amount -- the raw difference between interest earned and interest paid. In our example above, that is $9.8M.

**Net interest margin (NIM)** is the percentage -- that same dollar amount expressed as a share of earning assets. That is 3.16%.

Why does the distinction matter? Because NII alone does not tell you how efficiently you are deploying your balance sheet. A $1B credit union earning $28M in net interest income and a $350M credit union earning $9.8M have very different scales, but their NIMs might be identical. The percentage is what makes comparison possible -- across time periods, across peer groups, and across asset sizes.

When you hear "the credit union industry generated $80.4 billion in net interest income in 2025," that is an impressive dollar figure. But the number that tells you whether the industry is actually healthier is the NIM: 3.39% of average assets, up from 3.00% a year earlier. That 39-basis-point improvement, reported by NCUA, tells you the spread widened -- credit unions were keeping more of every dollar that moved through their balance sheets.

## Why Net Interest Margin Matters for Credit Unions

NIM is the clearest signal of your credit union's financial health for one simple reason: it measures how well your core business model is working. You take in money from members and the capital markets. You lend it out and invest it. The gap between what you pay and what you earn is NIM. If that gap shrinks, no amount of fee income or cost-cutting will save you long-term.

Credit unions have a unique tension here that banks do not face. The cooperative model means you exist to serve members, not to maximize profit. That creates a real pricing question: How competitive do you make your deposit rates? How aggressive are your loan rates? Every basis point you give away on the deposit side or the loan side compresses NIM. But if you price too aggressively in the other direction, you are not serving the mission.

The best-run credit unions I have worked with over 25 years navigate this by knowing their NIM intimately -- not as a quarterly number on a report, but as a daily pulse. They know exactly how much room they have to offer a competitive auto rate or a loyalty CD bonus without undercutting the spread that keeps the institution healthy. That precision comes from visibility, and visibility comes from frequency.

## What Is a Good Net Interest Margin?

"Good" depends on your cost structure, your market, and your membership base. But benchmarks help you calibrate.

**Credit union industry average (2025):** 3.39% of average assets, per NCUA data. That is up from 3.00% in 2024 -- a significant year-over-year improvement driven by easing deposit cost pressure and declining borrowing levels.

**By asset size, the picture shifts:**

- **$100M - $500M:** NIM tends to run slightly higher (3.40% - 3.65%) because smaller credit unions often have a higher proportion of relationship deposits at lower rates and concentrate more heavily in consumer lending with wider spreads.
- **$500M - $1B:** NIM typically falls in the 3.20% - 3.50% range as the deposit base grows more competitive and the loan mix diversifies.
- **$1B - $2B:** NIM often narrows to 3.00% - 3.35% as these institutions carry more complex funding structures, larger investment portfolios, and greater exposure to wholesale funding.

**Compared to commercial banks:** Large commercial banks often operate with NIMs in the 2.50% - 3.10% range. Credit unions generally maintain wider margins because of their lower cost of funds -- member deposits funded by relationship loyalty rather than rate competition alone.

The critical insight: do not chase a benchmark number. A credit union at 3.10% NIM with a clear understanding of why -- deliberate member-friendly pricing, a heavy investment portfolio, or a rate environment that temporarily compressed yields -- is in a stronger position than one at 3.50% NIM that does not know what is driving the number.

## What Causes NIM to Compress?

NIM compression is the scenario that keeps CFOs awake. Here are the three most common drivers:

**Rising deposit costs outpacing loan yield increases.** This is the classic squeeze. The Fed raises rates. Your members (and your competitors) demand higher share and CD rates. You reprice deposits upward. But your loan portfolio is slower to adjust -- fixed-rate mortgages do not reprice, auto loans are locked, and new originations take time to replace runoff. Your cost of funds climbs while your yield on earning assets barely moves. At a $500M credit union, a 25-basis-point cost-of-funds increase with flat loan yields costs $1.25M in annualized margin. That is the equivalent of six full-time salaries disappearing.

**Rate environment shifts.** When the yield curve flattens or inverts, credit unions that borrow short (paying rates tied to the short end of the curve) and lend long (earning rates tied to the long end) see their spread narrow. The 2022-2023 rate-hike cycle was a textbook example: short-term rates spiked while long-term rates rose more slowly, compressing the spread for institutions that had loaded up on long-duration assets during the low-rate era.

**Portfolio composition changes.** If your loan-to-asset ratio drops -- because originations slow or payoffs accelerate -- and those dollars move into lower-yielding investments, your NIM falls. A credit union that sees $2.4M in monthly loan runoff without matching origination volume is watching its highest-yielding assets walk out the door and get replaced by investment securities earning 150 to 200 basis points less.

Understanding what is causing compression is the first step. Catching it in time to act is the second -- and that depends entirely on how often you are looking. ## How Often Should You Be Looking at NIM?

Here is the question nobody on the first page of Google is asking, and it might be the most important one.

The standard cadence at most credit unions: NIM shows up in the monthly financial package, which is prepared 15 to 20 days after month-end, reviewed by the CFO, then presented to the board another week or two later. By the time anyone discusses it, the number is 30 to 45 days old.

Think about what can happen in 45 days. Deposit costs shifted because a competitor launched an aggressive money market promo. A large CD portfolio matured and repriced 60 basis points higher. Loan payoffs spiked because rates dropped and members refinanced. Each of those events moved your NIM -- and you did not know about it until the next board meeting.

I have sat in those meetings. I have watched a CFO present a NIM number that triggered concern, only to realize the underlying drivers had already shifted again by the time the board was seeing it. The discussion was about the past. The decisions that mattered were about right now.

- **NIM vs. NII:** NII is the dollar amount; NIM is the percentage. Use NIM for comparison and benchmarking.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a good net interest margin for a credit union?
The credit union industry average NIM was 3.39% of average assets in 2025, according to NCUA data. But "good" depends on context. A credit union at 3.10% that deliberately prices deposits competitively to serve its membership and maintains strong loan quality may be in a healthier position than one at 3.60% that is underpaying on shares and losing member relationships. Use the benchmark as a reference point, not a target.

### How do you calculate net interest margin?
The formula is: (Interest Income - Interest Expense) / Average Earning Assets. For a credit union with $18.2M in interest income, $8.4M in interest expense, and $310M in average earning assets, the NIM is ($18.2M - $8.4M) / $310M = 3.16%. Interest income includes loan interest, investment income, and fed funds sold. Interest expense includes share dividends, CD interest, and borrowing costs.

### What is the difference between net interest margin and net interest income?
Net interest income (NII) is the dollar amount -- the raw difference between what you earn on loans and investments and what you pay on deposits and borrowings. Net interest margin (NIM) is that same number expressed as a percentage of your average earning assets. NII tells you how many dollars you kept. NIM tells you how efficiently you kept them relative to the size of your balance sheet. NIM is the better comparison metric across institutions of different sizes.

### What causes net interest margin to decrease?
The three most common causes are: (1) deposit costs rising faster than loan yields increase -- the classic squeeze during rate-hike cycles; (2) yield curve shifts that narrow the spread between short-term borrowing costs and long-term lending rates; and (3) declining loan-to-asset ratios, where high-yielding loans run off and get replaced by lower-yielding investment securities. At a $500M credit union, a 25-basis-point shift in cost of funds with flat loan yields erases $1.25M in annualized margin.

### Why is net interest margin more important than ROA for credit unions?
ROA (return on assets) is a downstream number -- it includes non-interest income, operating expenses, and provision expense on top of net interest income. NIM isolates the core financial engine: how well you are buying and selling money. When NIM compresses, ROA will eventually follow no matter how much you cut costs or grow fee income. NIM is the leading indicator. ROA is the trailing one. Watch the engine, not just the dashboard.
