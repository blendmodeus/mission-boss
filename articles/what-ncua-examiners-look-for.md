# What NCUA Examiners Actually Look For: An Inside Look at the Examination Process

An examiner told me that the single most impressive thing a $400M credit union showed them was a 12-month log of every strategic decision — what data they reviewed, what they decided, and what happened next. The exam was completed two days early. Not because the credit union's numbers were perfect. Because the examiner could see, in documented evidence, that leadership understood its risks and was managing them with discipline.

That is what examiners actually look for. Not perfect numbers. Not zero findings. They look for evidence that your management team understands its credit union, makes informed decisions, monitors the results, and can prove all of it with documentation.

Most exam prep advice focuses on having the right documents ready. That matters — but it misses the bigger picture. Examiners are not checking boxes. They are evaluating management quality. And the credit unions that demonstrate that quality are the ones that run well every day, not just the ones that prepare well for the exam.

For the complete exam preparation framework, start with our [comprehensive NCUA exam prep guide](/resources/ncua-exam-preparation-guide).

## How Examiners Scope Your Exam

NCUA examinations are risk-focused. Examiners do not review every area of your credit union with equal intensity. Before they arrive, they have already determined where to spend their time based on several inputs.

**Your risk profile.** Call report data, financial trends, asset composition, and growth trajectory all inform scoping decisions. A credit union with 15% annual loan growth will receive deeper lending scrutiny than one growing at 3%.

**Prior exam history.** Unresolved findings from the previous exam automatically receive attention. If your last exam cited inadequate ALCO documentation, expect the examiner to go directly to your ALCO minutes and check whether you addressed it.

**Current supervisory priorities.** The annual [NCUA supervisory priorities letter](/resources/ncua-supervisory-priorities-2026) tells examiners where to focus. In 2026, that means balance sheet management, IRR, and cybersecurity will receive heightened attention across all credit unions, regardless of individual risk profiles.

**Peer comparison.** Examiners compare your metrics to peer groups — credit unions of similar asset size, charter type, and market. If your delinquency rate is 1.8% and peers average 0.9%, that gap triggers deeper review even if your rate is within your own policy limits.

The scoping process means your exam is tailored to you. That is both the opportunity and the challenge — you cannot predict exactly what the examiner will dig into, but you can make educated guesses based on your own risk profile and the published priorities.

## The First 48 Hours Set the Tone

This is the most under-discussed reality of the NCUA exam process. Examiners form early impressions — and those impressions influence the entire exam experience.

When documents are organized, submitted promptly through MERIT, and clearly labeled, the examiner starts with confidence that this is a well-managed credit union. When the point of contact responds to questions within hours rather than days, that reinforces the impression. When the credit union demonstrates during the entrance conference that leadership understands its own risk profile — not just the numbers, but the drivers behind them — the examiner's initial assessment tilts positive.

Contrast that with a credit union where the MERIT submission is incomplete and disorganized, the point of contact takes 48 hours to respond to basic questions, and the CEO cannot explain why NIM compressed 20 basis points in the last quarter. The examiner has not reviewed a single loan file yet, and the exam has already started on the wrong foot.

The first 48 hours are not about performance — they are about preparedness. And preparedness is something you build through daily discipline, not last-minute effort. Read our guide on [examiner-ready documentation](/resources/credit-union-examiner-documentation) for the specifics of how to organize your submission.

## Capital Adequacy: What Examiners Evaluate

Examiners assess whether your capital is sufficient to support your risk profile and growth plans.

**Net worth ratio trends.** Not just the current ratio — the trajectory over the last 8-12 quarters. A credit union at 9.5% net worth that was at 11% two years ago tells a different story than one that has held steady at 9.5%.

**Capital planning.** Does your capital policy include projections that tie to your strategic plan? If your strategic plan calls for 12% loan growth but your capital projections do not account for the resulting asset growth, examiners will note the disconnect.

**Concentration risk impact on capital.** If 40% of your loan portfolio is in commercial real estate, examiners want to see that your capital levels reflect the elevated risk concentration — not just the aggregate portfolio risk.

What signals strength: a documented capital planning process that connects directly to your strategic priorities, with projections that have been stress-tested under adverse conditions. What signals weakness: a capital policy that was last reviewed 18 months ago and projections that assume straight-line growth.

## Asset Quality: Beyond the Delinquency Numbers

Asset quality receives the most examination time for most credit unions, especially in 2026 with credit deterioration at its highest levels in over a decade.

**Underwriting standards vs. actual practice.** Examiners pull a sample of recent loans and compare them to your policy. They are looking for the gap between what your policy says and what your origination data shows. If your policy caps DTI at 43% but 25% of recent originations exceed that threshold, the follow-up question is: "Where is the documentation for each exception, and who approved it?"

**ACL adequacy.** Your Allowance for Credit Losses is one of the most scrutinized items in the exam. Examiners compare your ACL level to your actual loss history, your portfolio risk profile, and peer benchmarks. If your ACL methodology has not been updated to reflect current economic conditions, that is a finding.

**Loss mitigation effectiveness.** When loans go delinquent, what happens? Examiners want to see a documented, consistent process — not ad hoc workouts that vary by loan officer. They also want to see that your mitigation efforts are timely. A $35K auto loan that sits at 90 days delinquent for three months without documented contact and workout efforts signals weak collection practices.

**Charge-off timing.** NCUA has specific guidance on when loans should be charged off. Delayed charge-offs artificially inflate asset quality metrics. Examiners compare your charge-off timing to guidance and flag delays.

The examiner is asking one fundamental question: does this credit union's assessment of its own asset quality match reality? If your internal reports say asset quality is "satisfactory" but the data shows accelerating deterioration, that disconnect is a management concern — not just an asset quality concern.

## Management: The Most Subjective Component

The Management component is where examiners evaluate your leadership team's capability — not just whether the numbers are good, but whether management understands why they are good or bad and what they are doing about it.

**Strategic planning and execution.** Examiners review your strategic plan and ask: is this a living document connected to operations, or a binder that comes out for the exam? They look for evidence of execution tracking — documented progress on initiatives, adjustments made when conditions changed, accountability mechanisms. A strategic plan with a 2-week decision loop and a documented decision log is dramatically stronger evidence of management quality than a strategic plan with quarterly board updates.

**Risk management framework.** Can your CEO and CFO articulate the credit union's top three risks right now? Can they explain what data they use to monitor those risks and what decisions they have made in response? This is not a test of memorization — it is a test of whether leadership genuinely understands and manages its risk profile.

**Board governance.** Examiners assess whether the board is engaged and informed. Board minutes that document decisions with rationale demonstrate active governance. Board minutes that read like attendance logs demonstrate the opposite. Examiners also look at whether the board challenges management appropriately — rubber-stamping every recommendation is not governance.

**Internal controls and compliance.** Segregation of duties, dual controls, audit findings, and management's response to those findings all factor into the Management rating.

This component is where the daily discipline pays its biggest dividend. A leadership team that tracks its daily bottom line, reviews drivers and roadblocks, and documents decisions in 2-week loops can answer every management-quality question with documented evidence. That is not exam prep — that is how they run the credit union.

## Earnings: Sustainability, Not Just Profitability

Examiners do not just ask "are earnings good?" They ask "are earnings sustainable?"

**Net interest margin analysis.** NIM is the primary earnings metric examiners review. They look at the trend, the composition (loan yield vs. cost of funds), and the sustainability. A credit union that boosted NIM by loading up on high-yield unsecured consumer loans has a different risk profile than one that maintained NIM through disciplined pricing on indirect auto.

**The spread.** This is where daily visibility matters most. The spread between money bought and money sold is the engine of your credit union's earnings. Examiners want to see that you understand the components of your spread — what drives it up and what pushes it down — and that you monitor it with sufficient frequency to make timely decisions.

**Budget vs. actual performance.** How close did your actual results track to your projections? Significant variance — especially unexplained variance — raises questions about the accuracy of your planning assumptions and your management team's understanding of the business.

**Non-interest income and operating efficiency.** Fee income trends, operating expense ratios, and per-person productivity all factor into the earnings assessment. Examiners compare these metrics to peers to identify outliers that warrant further review.

## Liquidity: Can You Meet Your Obligations?

Liquidity assessment focuses on whether your credit union can meet its financial obligations under both normal and stressed conditions.

**Deposit concentration and stability.** If 20% of your deposits are held by five members, examiners will dig into your contingency planning for deposit concentration risk. How stable are those relationships? What happens if two of them leave?

**Contingency funding plan.** Having a CFP on file is the minimum. Examiners want to see evidence that you have tested it. When was the last time you confirmed your FHLB borrowing capacity? Have you drawn on your backup lines of credit to verify they function? An untested CFP is a plan, not a capability.

**Loan-to-share ratio trends.** A rising ratio signals that loan growth is outpacing deposit growth, which creates liquidity pressure. Examiners want to see that you are aware of the trend, monitoring it, and have a plan for managing it.

## Sensitivity to Market Risk: Where Credit Unions Struggle

The "S" in CAMELS is the newest component and the one most credit unions have the weakest documentation for.

**IRR model assumptions.** Examiners do not just review the model output — they review the assumptions that drive it. Non-maturity deposit assumptions (decay rates, repricing betas, surge balances) are particularly scrutinized because they have the largest impact on model results and are the hardest to validate. If your model assumes that savings accounts have a 7-year average life, you need data to support that assumption.

**Back-testing.** Did your model predictions from 12-18 months ago match what actually happened? Back-testing is the examiner's primary tool for assessing model reliability. If your model consistently overstates or understates actual results, your assumptions need recalibration — and examiners want to see that you identified and addressed the discrepancy.

**ALCO documentation.** ALCO minutes are the primary evidence for how your credit union manages sensitivity to market risk. Examiners look for documented discussions of model results, assumption validation, and decisions made in response to the analysis. Generic minutes that say "ALCO reviewed the report and found results acceptable" do not demonstrate management capability.

## The Red Flags That Trigger Deeper Examination

Certain indicators cause examiners to expand their review scope. Understanding these helps you anticipate where the examiner will focus.

**Rapid growth.** Asset growth above 10% annually, or any lending category growing significantly faster than the overall portfolio. Examiners want to verify that growth has not outpaced your risk management capabilities.

**Declining capital trends.** A net worth ratio trending downward over multiple quarters, even if still above the well-capitalized threshold, triggers deeper capital analysis.

**Policy exception patterns.** Occasional exceptions are normal. A pattern of exceptions — particularly in lending — suggests that your policies do not match your actual risk appetite. Examiners will ask whether your policies need updating or whether your practices need tightening.

**Management turnover.** A new CEO, CFO, or VP of Lending triggers deeper review because the examiner needs to assess whether the new leader understands the credit union's risk profile and has the capability to manage it.

**Prior findings not fully addressed.** This is one of the fastest paths to a Management component downgrade. If the previous exam cited a finding and your response was incomplete or ineffective, examiners view that as a management failure — you knew about the problem and did not fix it.

**Concentration risks.** Heavy concentration in any single loan type, industry, or geographic area increases portfolio risk and draws deeper examination.

For each of these red flags, the examiner is asking one question: does this credit union know this risk exists, and can it demonstrate that it is managing it?

## What Strong Management Looks Like to an Examiner

The credit unions that consistently receive the strongest examination results share three characteristics.

**They understand their own risks.** The CEO can explain, without preparation, the credit union's top three risks, what data they monitor, and what decisions they have made in response. This understanding comes from daily engagement with financial data, not from cramming before the exam.

**They document everything that matters.** Every strategic decision has a paper trail — data reviewed, alternatives considered, decision made, outcome monitored. Board minutes reflect active governance. ALCO minutes demonstrate genuine risk management. The decision log covers the full examination period.

**They close the loop.** Making a decision is half the work. Checking whether it worked is the other half. Examiners look for evidence that management follows through — that the decision to reprice CDs was implemented, that the impact was measured, and that the next decision was informed by the results.

These three characteristics — understanding, documentation, and follow-through — are exactly what a daily operating discipline produces. When you see your daily bottom line every morning, review your drivers and roadblocks, and document decisions in 2-week loops, you are building the management muscle that examiners evaluate.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What do NCUA examiners focus on?**

NCUA examiners focus on each component of the CAMELS rating: Capital adequacy, Asset quality, Management capability, Earnings sustainability, Liquidity position, and Sensitivity to market risk. The depth of review in each area depends on your credit union's risk profile, prior exam history, financial trends, and the current supervisory priorities. Examiners spend the most time on areas where they see elevated risk — rising delinquencies trigger deeper asset quality review, volatile earnings trigger deeper earnings and IRR analysis.

**How do NCUA examiners determine CAMELS ratings?**

Examiners evaluate each CAMELS component on a 1-5 scale (1 being strongest) based on quantitative metrics, qualitative assessment of management practices, and documented evidence of sound decision-making. The composite rating is not a simple average — it reflects the examiner's overall assessment of the credit union's safety and soundness. The Management component is the most subjective because it evaluates whether leadership understands its risks, makes informed decisions, and can demonstrate those decisions with documentation.

**What triggers an NCUA expanded exam?**

Red flags that trigger expanded examination scope include: rapid asset or loan growth (especially above 10% annually), declining capital trends, high or rising delinquency and charge-off rates, policy exception patterns suggesting loose underwriting, turnover in key management positions (CEO, CFO, lending), prior exam findings that have not been fully addressed, concentration risks in lending or deposits, volatile or declining earnings trends, and untested contingency funding plans. Each of these signals potential risk that warrants deeper review.

**How long does an NCUA exam take?**

Exam duration depends on asset size, complexity, and risk profile. A straightforward exam for a $150M credit union with a clean risk profile typically takes 2-3 weeks from document submission to exit conference. Larger credit unions ($500M+) or those with elevated risk factors may take 4-6 weeks. Credit unions that submit organized documentation promptly and respond to examiner questions quickly can shorten the timeline. Disorganized documentation and slow responses extend it — sometimes significantly.

**What are the most common NCUA exam findings?**

The most common findings include: insufficient documentation of board and committee decisions, stale policies not reviewed within their required cycle, ACL methodology that does not reflect current portfolio risk or economic conditions, inadequate IRR model assumption documentation and back-testing, underwriting exceptions without documented rationale, incomplete vendor management oversight, and weak BSA risk assessments that do not match the credit union's actual risk profile. Most of these findings are documentation and process issues, not financial performance problems.
