# Credit Union Liquidity Management: Planning for What's Next

A credit union can survive compressed margins. It can survive a tough exam. It cannot survive running out of cash.

Liquidity risk does not get the headlines that interest rate risk does. It is not the topic that fills conference agendas or dominates ALCO presentations. But it is the risk that can shut your doors in a matter of weeks if it is mismanaged -- and in the current environment, it is the risk that is catching mid-size credit unions off guard.

I have worked with credit unions that had solid capital ratios, healthy loan portfolios, and clean exam histories -- and still found themselves scrambling to fund loan demand because their deposit base was quietly eroding. The numbers looked fine in the monthly report. The problem was that the monthly report was already 30 days old when anyone saw it.

Liquidity management is not about holding enough cash to feel comfortable. It is about knowing -- every day -- whether your cash inflows match your cash outflows, and having a plan for when they do not. This guide covers what credit union leaders between $100M and $2B need to build a liquidity program that actually works, not one that just passes exam.

## Why Liquidity Risk Is Front and Center Right Now

The liquidity environment for credit unions has fundamentally shifted since 2020, and the pressure has not let up.

**Deposit runoff is real and accelerating.** After years of pandemic-era deposit growth that made liquidity feel effortless, credit unions have watched members move money. Share growth across the industry turned negative in 2023 for the first time in decades. High-yield savings accounts at fintechs, Treasury direct purchases, and aggressive CD pricing from banks have given members more options -- and more reasons to move.

A $600M credit union I partnered with tracked $4.8M in net deposit outflows over a single quarter in late 2025. That was not a crisis by itself, but it was a trend that would have consumed their excess liquidity within six months if it continued. They caught it through daily monitoring. A credit union relying on monthly reports would have seen the first month's numbers six weeks after the fact.

**Loan demand has created funding pressure.** Credit unions that leaned into auto lending and first mortgages during 2023-2025 found themselves funding loan growth while deposits shrank. That means tapping wholesale funding sources -- FHLB advances, brokered deposits, correspondent lines -- at costs significantly higher than member deposits.

**NCUA's 2026 supervisory priorities call out liquidity explicitly.** The supervisory priorities letter highlights deposit trends, concentration risk, and contingency funding plan adequacy as examination focus areas. Examiners are paying closer attention to how credit unions are managing liquidity in a higher-rate, lower-deposit-growth environment.

**Delinquency trends are adding a second layer of pressure.** Rising delinquencies and charge-offs reduce cash inflows from loan payments while simultaneously requiring higher ACL reserve levels. A credit union dealing with deposit runoff and rising delinquencies faces a two-front liquidity challenge that demands daily attention.

## NCUA Requirements You Need to Know

NCUA's liquidity requirements are spelled out in 12 CFR 741.12 and the Liquidity Risk Management Advisory. Here is what they mean for your credit union.

### Written Liquidity Policy

Every federally insured credit union must maintain a written liquidity policy approved by the board. The policy must address:

- **Risk tolerance.** How much liquidity risk is acceptable? Define specific limits -- minimum on-balance-sheet liquidity ratios, maximum wholesale funding concentrations, trigger points for escalation.
- **Measurement and monitoring.** What metrics do you track and how often? At minimum: cash-to-assets ratio, loan-to-share ratio, available borrowing capacity, and non-core funding dependence.
- **Roles and responsibilities.** Who monitors liquidity daily? Who has authority to access contingency funding sources? Who notifies the board?
- **Reporting requirements.** What does the board see, and how often?

### Contingency Funding Plan

Credit unions with assets over $50M must maintain a contingency funding plan. This is not a document you write once and file. It is a plan your team should be able to execute under pressure. NCUA expects it to include:

- Identified contingency funding sources with specific dollar amounts
- Time-to-access for each source (same day, next day, within a week)
- Tiered triggers that activate escalating response levels
- Communication and escalation protocols
- Roles and responsibilities during a liquidity event
- Testing cadence (at minimum annually, preferably semi-annually)

### CAMELS "L" Component

Examiners evaluate liquidity under the "L" component of your CAMELS rating. They are looking for evidence that your credit union can meet current and anticipated cash flow needs without significant cost or disruption. A 1 or 2 rating means your liquidity management is sound. A 3 means there are weaknesses that need attention. A 4 or 5 means there are serious concerns.

The most common findings that push a rating from 2 to 3:

- Contingency funding plan that has not been tested or updated
- Over-concentration in a single wholesale funding source
- No early warning indicators defined or monitored
- Liquidity projections built entirely on stale monthly data
- Board reporting that lacks meaningful liquidity metrics

## Your Sources of Liquidity

Liquidity sources fall into two categories, and understanding the depth and accessibility of each is fundamental to sound management.

### On-Balance-Sheet Sources

These are the most reliable and least costly sources:

- **Cash and cash equivalents.** Your first line of defense. Immediately available with no cost to access.
- **Unpledged investments.** Securities that can be sold or used as collateral. Know the market value, not just the book value -- in a rising-rate environment, unrealized losses on your investment portfolio reduce the actual liquidity value of those holdings.
- **Maturing loans.** Scheduled principal and interest payments from your loan portfolio provide predictable cash inflows. For a $400M credit union with a $280M loan portfolio, monthly principal and interest cash flows might run $3.5M-$4.5M -- significant liquidity that is easy to overlook.

### Off-Balance-Sheet Sources

These are your backup lines -- available but at a cost:

- **Federal Home Loan Bank (FHLB) lines.** Most credit unions have FHLB borrowing capacity. Know your available limit, the current cost, and how quickly you can draw. A $300M credit union with $50M in FHLB capacity has a meaningful backstop, but only if the team knows how to access it quickly and has tested the process.
- **Central Liquidity Facility (CLF).** NCUA's Central Liquidity Facility provides emergency lending to credit unions. It is a last-resort source, and using it signals stress -- but knowing it exists and understanding the access process is part of sound planning.
- **Correspondent credit union lines.** Reciprocal lending arrangements with other credit unions provide diversified contingency funding. These relationships need to be maintained and tested, not just documented.
- **Brokered deposits.** Available as a funding source but at higher cost and with regulatory scrutiny if you become over-reliant. NCUA pays close attention to brokered deposit concentrations, especially for credit unions that are not well-capitalized.

### Tiering Your Sources

Build a tiered framework:

- **Tier 1 (daily operations):** Cash, overnight investments, scheduled loan payments
- **Tier 2 (moderate stress):** Unpledged investment sales, FHLB advances, CD specials to retain deposits
- **Tier 3 (severe stress):** CLF access, brokered deposits, asset sales, correspondent lines

Know the dollar amount available in each tier, the cost to access, and the time required. Update these figures quarterly at minimum.

## Building a Contingency Funding Plan That Actually Works

Most contingency funding plans I have reviewed check the regulatory box without being operationally useful. The difference between a plan that passes exam and a plan that saves your credit union comes down to specificity and testing.

### Make It Specific

Instead of "we will access FHLB borrowing capacity," your plan should state: "We have $42M in available FHLB advance capacity as of March 2026. The CFO can initiate a draw by calling our FHLB relationship manager at [phone number]. Funds are available same-day for overnight advances, next-day for term advances. Current overnight rate: 5.15%."

That level of specificity means someone can execute the plan under stress without stopping to figure out how it works.

### Define Tiered Triggers

Your plan should have clear escalation levels:

- **Watch level:** Deposit outflows exceed 2% in a rolling 30-day period, or loan-to-share ratio exceeds 95%. Action: increase monitoring frequency, alert CFO.
- **Elevated level:** Deposit outflows exceed 5% in a rolling 30-day period, or available liquidity falls below policy minimums. Action: activate Tier 2 funding sources, alert CEO and board chair.
- **Critical level:** Multiple funding sources constrained simultaneously, or deposit outflows exceed 10% in a rolling 30-day period. Action: activate Tier 3 sources, full board notification, consider reducing new loan commitments.

### Test It

Run a tabletop exercise at least annually. Present your leadership team with a scenario -- "a competitor just launched a 5.25% CD special and we are seeing $200K per day in share certificate withdrawals" -- and walk through the plan step by step. Time the exercise. Identify gaps. Document what you learn. This testing is exactly the kind of evidence examiners want to see.

## Cash Flow Projections: Getting Ahead of the Numbers

Liquidity management runs on cash flow projections. If your projections are built on monthly data, they are already stale when you receive them.

### What Sound Projections Include

- **Loan payment inflows.** Scheduled principal and interest, adjusted for delinquency trends and prepayment assumptions.
- **Deposit inflows and outflows.** New account openings, share certificate maturities, seasonal patterns, and the steady-state flow of member transactions.
- **Investment maturities and calls.** Cash coming back from the investment portfolio on a scheduled basis.
- **Operating expenses.** Payroll, occupancy, technology, and other recurring costs.
- **Loan funding commitments.** Approved but unfunded loans, mortgage pipeline, and lines of credit.
- **Wholesale funding maturities.** When do your FHLB advances and brokered CDs come due?

### The Freshness Problem

Here is where most credit unions fall short: they build these projections off month-end data and update them monthly. That means every day between updates, the projection is getting less accurate.

A $350M credit union I worked with discovered this the hard way. Their monthly cash flow projection showed comfortable liquidity for the next 90 days. But in the three weeks since the data was compiled, deposit outflows had accelerated by $2.4M beyond what the projection assumed. A competitor had launched an aggressive money market promotion, and members were moving funds. By the time the next monthly projection was built, the credit union had burned through most of its excess liquidity cushion.

Daily cash flow visibility would have surfaced that deposit movement within the first week. Instead, the team spent three weeks operating on outdated projections.

## Early Warning Indicators and Stress Testing

### Key Metrics to Monitor

Build a dashboard of early warning indicators that your team reviews at least weekly -- daily is better:

- **Loan-to-share ratio.** Trending above 90% signals that loan growth is outpacing deposit growth. Above 100% means you are funding loans with non-member sources.
- **Non-core funding dependence.** Wholesale funding (FHLB, brokered deposits) exceeding 20% of total funding warrants increased monitoring.
- **Net short-term liabilities.** Liabilities maturing within 30 days minus assets maturing within 30 days. A widening gap means increasing short-term funding pressure.
- **Available borrowing capacity utilization.** If you have drawn more than 60% of your available borrowing lines, your remaining backup is thinner than it looks.
- **Deposit concentration.** Any single depositor or deposit type exceeding 5% of total deposits creates concentration risk.
- **Month-over-month deposit trends.** Three consecutive months of net deposit outflows is a pattern, not an anomaly.

### Stress Testing

Your liquidity stress testing should model scenarios that are plausible and painful:

- **Deposit shock.** What happens if 10% of your non-maturity deposits leave within 30 days? Can you fund operations and meet loan demand?
- **Funding access disruption.** What if your primary wholesale funding source is unavailable? Do you have diversified alternatives?
- **Combined stress.** Deposit outflows plus rising delinquencies plus an unexpected loan commitment draw. The real world delivers multiple stresses simultaneously.

For each scenario, document the cash flow impact, the funding sources you would access, the cost, and the timeline. Present the results to the board in dollar terms: "Under this scenario, we would need $12M in additional funding within 30 days at an estimated cost of $185K above our current cost of funds."

## The Daily Visibility Advantage

Here is the practical difference between monthly and daily liquidity monitoring.

With monthly data, your liquidity management follows this cycle: month-end closes, data is compiled over 10-15 business days, the report is distributed, and the ALCO reviews it at the next meeting. If deposit runoff started on day three of the month, you might not discuss it for six weeks. By then, the trend has either stabilized (lucky) or compounded (expensive).

With daily visibility, the cycle is: today's numbers are available tomorrow morning. Deposit outflows show up in real time. Loan funding patterns are visible as they happen. The spread between money bought and money sold tells you immediately whether today's cash flow position is better or worse than yesterday.

A $450M credit union that moved to daily cash flow monitoring spotted a pattern: every Friday following a competitor's weekly rate email blast, they were seeing $80K-$120K in share certificate early withdrawals. Over a month, that was $400K-$500K in deposit runoff tied to a single competitor action. Monthly reporting would have shown the aggregate outflow but would never have connected it to the timing pattern. Daily visibility let them respond with a targeted retention offer within two weeks.

That is the difference between reactive liquidity management and proactive liquidity management.

## Connecting Liquidity Decisions to Your Strategic Plan

Liquidity management is not just a compliance exercise. The liquidity decisions you make today directly shape your credit union's strategic capacity tomorrow.

**Loan pricing depends on funding cost.** If your marginal cost of funds is 4.8% because you are relying on wholesale funding to cover deposit runoff, your loan pricing floor just moved. Every loan you book below that marginal cost is diluting your spread. Daily visibility into funding costs makes this connection explicit.

**Deposit strategy drives balance sheet capacity.** The credit unions that are growing sustainably in this environment are the ones that treat deposit retention as a strategic priority, not a back-office function. A $500M credit union that invested $150,000 in a targeted deposit retention campaign -- triggered by daily monitoring that showed accelerating share outflows -- retained $3.1M in deposits that would have otherwise moved to a competitor. The return on that investment was measurable within 30 days.

**Balance sheet positioning requires current information.** Every decision about loan growth targets, investment portfolio duration, and wholesale funding usage is a liquidity decision. Making those decisions on 30-day-old data is like driving with a fogged windshield. You can see the general direction, but the details that matter -- the ones that keep you out of the ditch -- are blurry.

Liquidity insights should feed directly into your credit union's strategic execution framework. In tight two-week decision cycles, your team can spot a deposit trend, decide on a response, execute the response, and measure the result -- all before the monthly report would have surfaced the original trend. That is liquidity management as a strategic discipline, not a compliance checkbox.

## FAQ: Credit Union Liquidity Management

### What are the NCUA liquidity requirements for credit unions?

NCUA requires all federally insured credit unions to maintain a written, board-approved liquidity policy under 12 CFR 741.12. Credit unions with assets over $50M must also maintain a contingency funding plan that identifies specific funding sources, access procedures, tiered triggers, and escalation protocols. The board must review and approve the liquidity policy at least annually. Examiners evaluate liquidity under the "L" component of the CAMELS rating, looking for evidence that the credit union can meet current and anticipated cash flow needs without significant cost or disruption to operations.

### What is a contingency funding plan for credit unions?

A contingency funding plan (CFP) is a documented strategy for accessing additional liquidity under stress conditions. A sound CFP identifies specific funding sources (FHLB advances, Central Liquidity Facility, correspondent lines, investment liquidation, brokered deposits) with dollar amounts available from each, time to access, and current cost. It defines tiered triggers that activate escalating response levels, communication protocols for board and management notification, and testing procedures. NCUA expects credit unions over $50M to maintain and periodically test their CFP.

### What are the warning signs of a credit union liquidity problem?

Key early warning indicators include three or more consecutive months of net deposit outflows, a loan-to-share ratio exceeding 95% and trending upward, non-core funding (FHLB, brokered deposits) exceeding 20% of total funding, available borrowing capacity utilization above 60%, rising share certificate early withdrawal volumes, and deposit concentration where a single depositor or deposit type exceeds 5% of total deposits. Any single indicator warrants increased monitoring. Multiple indicators appearing simultaneously require immediate attention and potential activation of the contingency funding plan.

### How do credit unions calculate cash flow projections?

Cash flow projections model expected inflows (loan principal and interest payments, investment maturities, new deposit accounts, fee income) against expected outflows (share withdrawals, loan fundings, operating expenses, borrowing maturities, capital expenditures) over 30, 60, and 90-day horizons. Sound projections adjust for delinquency trends, seasonal patterns, prepayment assumptions, and pipeline commitments. The key challenge is data freshness -- projections built on monthly data become less accurate every day. Credit unions that update projections with daily cash flow data can identify emerging gaps weeks before monthly projections would surface them.

### How does liquidity management connect to interest rate risk?

Liquidity risk and interest rate risk are the two core components of asset liability management, and they interact constantly. Rising rates can trigger deposit outflows as members chase higher yields, creating liquidity pressure. Falling rates can accelerate mortgage prepayments, changing the timing and amount of asset-side cash flows. Investment portfolios with unrealized losses from rate increases have reduced liquidity value. A sound ALM program manages both risks together through the ALCO process. For a deeper look at the interest rate risk side, see our guide to [interest rate risk management for credit unions](/resources/interest-rate-risk-management-credit-unions).
