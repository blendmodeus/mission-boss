Net Interest Margin for Credit Unions: Complete Guide

The definitive guide to net interest margin for credit union leaders. NIM formula, 2025 benchmarks, compression drivers, and 7 strategies to improve your spread.

The Complete Guide to Net Interest Margin for Credit Unions

Every credit union CEO, CFO, and board member has a number that defines whether their institution is moving forward or slowly bleeding out. That number is net interest margin. Not loan growth. Not membership count. Not the efficiency ratio. Those are symptoms. Net interest margin is the engine.

I have spent 25 years inside and alongside credit unions, and I have watched NIM compress 40 basis points in a single quarter because deposit costs moved faster than anyone expected while loan repricing lagged behind. I have sat in board meetings where the NIM being discussed was already six weeks old, the underlying dynamics had shifted, and the room was debating a number that no longer reflected reality. And I have seen what happens when a leadership team gets daily visibility into their spread -- how the conversations change, how the decisions sharpen, and how the margin responds.

This guide is everything I know about net interest margin, written for the people who actually manage it. Not a textbook definition. Not a formula you memorize for a certification exam. A practical, operator-level breakdown of the number that funds everything your credit union does.

What Is Net Interest Margin?

Net interest margin is the spread between money bought and money sold, expressed as a percentage of your earning assets. That is it. Every dollar your credit union takes in -- through share accounts, CDs, money markets, borrowings -- has a cost. Every dollar you lend out or invest has a yield. The gap between those two rates, normalized against your earning asset base, is your NIM.

The formula:

NIM = (Interest Income - Interest Expense) / Average Earning Assets

The components:

  • Interest income: Everything your credit union earns from loans (auto, mortgage, consumer, commercial), investment securities, and other interest-earning assets.
  • Interest expense: Everything you pay for funding -- share dividends, CD interest, money market rates, and the cost of borrowings from the FHLB or other sources.
  • Average earning assets: The denominator. This includes your total loan portfolio, investment portfolio, deposits at other institutions, and fed funds sold. It excludes non-earning assets like buildings, equipment, and NCUSIF deposits.

Clearing Up the Terminology: NIM vs. NII vs. Spread

These three terms get tangled constantly. Here is the distinction:

  • Net interest income (NII) is the dollar amount. Interest earned minus interest paid. If you earned $18.2M and paid $8.4M, your NII is $9.8M.
  • Net interest margin (NIM) is the percentage. That $9.8M divided by your $310M in average earning assets gives you 3.16%.
  • Spread is used more loosely. Some people use it interchangeably with NIM. Others use it to describe the gap between a specific loan rate and a specific funding rate. In this guide, when I say "the spread between money bought and money sold," I mean NIM.

Why Net Interest Margin Is the Number That Matters Most

NIM is the single clearest signal of whether your credit union's core business model is working. You exist to take in deposits and deploy them as loans and investments. The margin on that activity is NIM. When it expands, you have room to invest in people, technology, member services, and reserves. When it compresses, everything gets squeezed -- and no amount of fee income innovation or expense cutting will compensate for a fundamentally broken spread.

The Cooperative Model Tension

Here is where credit unions face a challenge that banks do not. The cooperative model means you serve members, not shareholders. That creates real pressure on both sides of the NIM equation:

  • On the deposit side: Members expect competitive rates. Your board may push for higher share dividends to demonstrate member value. Every basis point you add to deposit rates compresses NIM.
  • On the loan side: Members expect affordable borrowing. Offering the best auto loan rate in your market is good for membership growth but narrows the spread on your highest-yielding asset class.

The tension is real and it is constant. The credit unions that navigate it best are the ones that know their margin with precision -- daily precision -- so they can make intentional tradeoffs rather than discovering the impact after the fact.

How NIM Flows Downstream

NIM is not just a standalone metric. It feeds directly into:

  • Return on assets (ROA): NIM is the largest component of revenue for most credit unions. When NIM drops, ROA follows.
  • Net worth ratio: Sustained NIM compression reduces retained earnings, which erodes your capital cushion over time.
  • Long-term sustainability: A credit union with a structurally low NIM cannot invest in growth, absorb losses, or serve members through the next downturn.

The point is not to maximize NIM at the expense of everything else. It is to manage it intentionally, with full visibility, so that every basis point you give away is a choice -- not a surprise.

How to Calculate Net Interest Margin Step by Step

Let's walk through a real calculation using a $350M credit union.

Step 1: Calculate total interest income.

Source Average Balance Yield Annual Income
Loans (all types) $245M 5.95% $14,577,500
Investments $55M 4.15% $2,282,500
Other earning assets $10M 5.25% $525,000
Total $310M $17,385,000

Step 2: Calculate total interest expense.

Source Average Balance Rate Annual Expense
Regular shares $105M 0.45% $472,500
Money markets $70M 3.80% $2,660,000
CDs $85M 4.45% $3,782,500
Borrowings (FHLB) $25M 5.05% $1,262,500
Total $285M $8,177,500

Step 3: Compute net interest income.

$17,385,000 - $8,177,500 = $9,207,500

Step 4: Divide by average earning assets.

$9,207,500 / $310,000,000 = 2.97%

This credit union is running a 2.97% NIM -- below the 2025 industry average of 3.39%. The immediate question for leadership: why, and what can we move?

Common Calculation Mistakes

  • Using total assets instead of earning assets. Total assets includes your building, your NCUSIF deposit, and your fixed assets. Those do not earn interest. Using total assets deflates your NIM and makes comparisons meaningless.
  • Mixing time periods. If you calculate interest income for a quarter but use an annual average balance, your NIM will be wrong. Match the periods.
  • Ignoring non-member deposits or off-balance-sheet items. Make sure your denominator captures all earning assets and your numerator captures all interest-related income and expense.

What Is a Good Net Interest Margin?

The credit union industry reported a NIM of 3.39% of average assets in 2025, up from 3.00% in 2024, according to NCUA quarterly data. That 39-basis-point improvement was the largest year-over-year gain in over a decade, driven by easing deposit cost pressure as the rate-hike cycle plateaued and expensive borrowings matured off balance sheets. Total industry net interest income hit $80.4 billion, a 12.9% year-over-year increase.

NIM by Asset Size

Asset size meaningfully affects NIM:

  • $100M - $500M: These credit unions typically maintain NIMs of 3.40% to 3.65%. Their funding base skews toward lower-cost relationship deposits, and their loan mix concentrates in higher-spread consumer products (auto, personal, credit cards). Less reliance on wholesale funding helps.
  • $500M - $1B: NIMs generally run 3.20% to 3.50%. Deposit competition intensifies at this scale, and the loan mix often diversifies into lower-spread products like first mortgages and commercial real estate.
  • $1B - $2B: NIMs narrow to 3.00% to 3.35%. These institutions carry more complex balance sheets, larger investment portfolios, and greater exposure to wholesale funding and borrowings that carry higher costs.

Credit Unions vs. Commercial Banks

Credit unions generally maintain wider NIMs than large commercial banks, which often operate in the 2.50% to 3.10% range. The primary reason: credit unions fund themselves predominantly with member deposits at relationship-driven rates, while large banks rely more heavily on wholesale funding, brokered deposits, and market-rate instruments. That structural funding advantage is one of the cooperative model's genuine strengths -- but only if you are watching it closely enough to protect it.

Why "Good" Depends on Context

A credit union at 3.10% NIM that deliberately offers best-in-market deposit rates as a member value proposition and maintains pristine loan quality is not "worse" than a peer at 3.50% that is underpaying on shares and tolerating higher credit risk. The number matters, but the intent behind it matters more. What separates strong institutions from struggling ones is whether the NIM outcome was a choice or a surprise.

The Drivers of Net Interest Margin

NIM does not move on its own. Specific, identifiable forces push it up or pull it down. Understanding these drivers is the difference between reacting to NIM changes and managing them.

Loan Yield

Your loan portfolio is the largest earning asset and the primary source of interest income. Loan yield is driven by:

  • Mix: Auto loans at 6.50% produce a different yield profile than 30-year fixed mortgages at 5.00%. A shift in your origination mix changes the blended yield.
  • Pricing: How aggressively you price to win volume. Dropping your auto rate 25 basis points to compete might boost originations but compresses yield on the entire flow.
  • Vintage turnover: As older, lower-rate loans pay off and get replaced by new originations at current market rates, your blended yield adjusts. This can work for or against you depending on the direction of rates.

Cost of Funds

The other side of the spread. Your cost of funds is driven by your deposit mix (low-cost checking vs. rate-sensitive CDs), your pricing decisions, and your reliance on borrowings. This is typically the most volatile NIM driver because deposit costs respond faster to rate changes than loan yields do. When the Fed moved rates up 525 basis points in 2022-2023, credit unions watched their cost of funds spike while large portions of their loan portfolios sat at fixed rates from the low-rate era, unable to reprice.

Asset Composition

The percentage of your total assets deployed in earning assets matters. A credit union with 88% of assets earning interest has a structural NIM advantage over one at 82%. Non-earning assets -- buildings, equipment, NCUSIF deposits, cash reserves above what is needed -- dilute the denominator without contributing to the numerator.

Rate Environment

Rising rates, falling rates, and flat rates each affect NIM differently:

  • Rising rates: Initially compress NIM because deposit costs rise faster than loan yields adjust. Over time, NIM can recover as new loans are booked at higher rates.
  • Falling rates: Can expand NIM in the short term if deposit costs drop faster than loan yields. But if borrowers refinance high-rate loans, you lose your best-yielding assets.
  • Flat rates: Provide the most stable NIM environment, but can mask underlying shifts in deposit mix or loan composition that erode margin slowly.

Prepayment Risk

When rates drop, borrowers refinance. Your 6.25% mortgage portfolio starts paying off faster than expected, and those dollars get redeployed into new originations at 4.75% or parked in investments yielding even less. Prepayment risk is the hidden NIM killer in falling-rate environments. A $250M mortgage portfolio that experiences 15% faster prepayment speeds than modeled can cost $400,000 or more in annualized income as high-yield assets leave the books early.

NIM Across Rate Cycles: A 25-Year Perspective

I have watched NIM behave through every major rate cycle of the last quarter century. The patterns are instructive.

2001-2003 (Post-dot-com, rate cuts to 1%): NIM initially expanded as deposit costs dropped faster than loan yields. But the extended low-rate environment eventually compressed margins as the loan portfolio fully repriced down and deposit costs hit a floor.

2008-2010 (Financial crisis, emergency rate cuts): Credit unions with heavy exposure to long-term fixed-rate mortgages initially benefited as funding costs plummeted. But refinancing waves eroded the high-yield portfolio, and the extended near-zero rate era that followed squeezed margins for years.

2010-2020 (The long low-rate era): A decade of compressed NIM. Credit unions stretched for yield by extending loan duration, buying longer investment securities, and entering new lending verticals. Cost of funds could not go much lower, but loan yields kept declining with each new origination vintage.

2022-2023 (Aggressive rate hikes, 525 bps): The most disruptive cycle in a generation. Deposit costs spiked as CD specials and money market promotions proliferated. Loan portfolios, loaded with low-rate fixed assets from the prior decade, could not reprice fast enough. NIM compressed sharply at many credit unions -- some losing 40 to 60 basis points in a single year. Borrowings from the FHLB surged, adding expensive wholesale funding to balance sheets.

2024-2025 (Stabilization and recovery): As the rate environment plateaued and then began easing, deposit cost pressure moderated. Expensive borrowings matured and rolled off -- industry borrowings declined from 6.0% to 5.2% of assets. New loan originations at higher rates started replacing the low-rate vintages. The industry NIM recovered to 3.39%, up from 3.00%.

The pattern: NIM gets hit hardest when rate changes are sudden and directional. The lag between when your cost of funds moves and when your loan yields adjust is where the damage happens. Credit unions that see the shift early -- daily rather than monthly -- can act before the full impact hits.

The Cost of Seeing NIM 30-45 Days Late

This is the operational reality that no textbook covers and no competing article on the first page of Google addresses.

At most credit unions, the NIM calculation happens during the monthly financial close. The accounting team runs the numbers 15 to 20 business days after month-end. The CFO reviews them. The ALM or finance committee reviews them. The board sees them two weeks later. By the time anyone with decision-making authority discusses the NIM, it is 30 to 45 days old.

Here is what that delay costs you in a real scenario:

A $450M credit union is running a 3.25% NIM. During the first two weeks of the month, three things happen: (1) a competitor launches a 4.75% money market promo, pulling $8M in deposits that the credit union has to replace with FHLB borrowings at 5.30%; (2) a $15M pool of 18-month CDs matures and the renewal rate jumps from 3.90% to 4.50%; and (3) auto loan originations slow because the credit union's rate is 50 basis points above a competing bank.

Each of those events compresses NIM. Combined, they represent roughly 8 to 12 basis points of margin pressure -- $360,000 to $540,000 in annualized impact on a $450M balance sheet. But leadership does not see any of this until mid-next-month at the earliest. By then, the CD has already repriced. The borrowing is already on the books. The auto loan volume has already been lost.

The delay is not just an inconvenience. It is a structural disadvantage. You cannot manage what you cannot see in time to act on it.

Seven Strategies to Improve Net Interest Margin

Improving NIM is not about one big move. It is about consistent, informed decisions across multiple levers. Here are the seven strategies that matter most for credit unions in the current environment.

1. Reprice Maturing Deposits Aggressively but Strategically

When a $10M batch of 12-month CDs matures at 4.65%, you have a decision window. What is the renewal rate? Do you match the competitor down the street at 4.25%, or do you hold at 3.90% and accept some runoff? The answer depends on your current funding position and your cost of funds trajectory -- information you need today, not from last month's report. A credit union that reprices $40M in maturing CDs 35 basis points lower saves $140,000 annually.

2. Pay Down Expensive Borrowings as They Mature

Industry-wide, credit unions reduced borrowings from 6.0% to 5.2% of assets in 2025. The credit unions that moved fastest replaced FHLB advances at 5.00% or higher with deposit growth at 3.50% or lower -- a 150-basis-point improvement on every dollar shifted. On $20M in borrowings replaced with deposits, that is $300,000 in annual interest expense saved.

3. Shift Loan Mix Toward Higher-Yield Categories

Not all loans are created equal from a margin perspective. A $5M shift from 30-year fixed mortgages at 5.25% to indirect auto loans at 6.50% adds 125 basis points of yield on that volume -- $62,500 annually. The key is doing this intentionally, not accidentally, and understanding the risk tradeoffs involved.

4. Adopt Relationship-Based Deposit Pricing

A member with a checking account, auto loan, credit card, and direct deposit costs far less to retain than a rate-shopping CD holder. Price accordingly. Offer loyalty CD rates 15 to 25 basis points below your promotional rate to members with three or more products. You keep the relationship. You lower your cost of funds. Your NIM benefits.

5. Monitor and Manage Prepayment Risk

If rates are declining, your highest-yielding assets -- mortgages, longer-term consumer loans -- will start paying off faster than expected. Model the impact. If your $180M mortgage portfolio accelerates from 8% CPR to 12% CPR, that is an additional $7.2M in annual payoffs from high-yield assets that get replaced by lower-yield originations or investments. Know this before it shows up in the numbers.

6. Move from Monthly to Daily NIM Awareness

This is the strategy multiplier. Every lever above works better when you can see the impact in near-real-time. Repriced that CD batch? See the cost-of-funds impact tomorrow. Paid down a borrowing? Watch the expense line adjust. Lost auto volume to a competitor? See the yield shift before it becomes a quarterly trend. Daily visibility does not create more work -- it makes the work you are already doing more precise.

7. Document Every Margin-Impacting Decision

When an NCUA examiner asks how you manage interest-rate risk, showing a documented trail of margin-impacting decisions -- with dates, data, and rationale -- is fundamentally different from saying "we review NIM quarterly." Documentation creates accountability, and accountability improves decision quality over time. Watch how credit unions using Mission BOSS improved their margins through documented decision-making.

NIM and NCUA Exams: What Examiners Look For

NCUA examiners are not just checking that you know your NIM. They are evaluating whether your institution has a disciplined, documented process for managing it.

What examiners evaluate:

  • Margin trends over time. Is your NIM stable, improving, or deteriorating? If deteriorating, do you know why and what you are doing about it?
  • Interest rate risk management. How does your NIM perform under stressed rate scenarios? Have you modeled the impact of a 200-basis-point shift in both directions?
  • Documentation. Can you show that leadership reviewed NIM trends and made documented decisions in response? A board resolution or committee minute that says "reviewed NIM" is not the same as a documented decision trail that shows "NIM compressed 12 bps due to CD repricing; approved strategy to shift $15M in maturing CDs to lower rate tier, expected to recover 8 bps over 90 days."

Why "we review it quarterly" is no longer sufficient. The rate environment of the last several years has demonstrated that NIM can shift materially within a single month. Examiners increasingly expect credit unions to demonstrate more frequent monitoring and faster response cycles. A quarterly review cadence worked when rates were stable for a decade. It does not work when your cost of funds can move 20 basis points between board meetings.

The credit unions that perform best in exams are the ones that can show examiners a real-time picture of their margin, a documented history of decisions made in response to margin changes, and a clear process for how leadership stays informed. That is not a compliance exercise -- it is sound management. But it also happens to be exactly what examiners want to see.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the average net interest margin for credit unions?

The credit union industry average NIM was 3.39% of average assets in 2025, according to NCUA data. This was up significantly from 3.00% in 2024, reflecting easing deposit cost pressure and declining reliance on expensive borrowings. NIM varies meaningfully by asset size: smaller credit unions ($100M-$500M) typically run 3.40% to 3.65%, while larger institutions ($1B-$2B) often see 3.00% to 3.35%.

How do you improve net interest margin at a credit union?

The most effective strategies are: repricing maturing deposits aggressively, paying down expensive borrowings as they mature, shifting loan origination mix toward higher-spread products, adopting relationship-based deposit pricing that rewards loyal members, managing prepayment risk in your mortgage portfolio, and moving from monthly to daily NIM monitoring so you can act on shifts before they compound. No single lever moves NIM dramatically -- it is the consistent, informed combination of all of them.

What is the difference between net interest margin and spread?

In practice, many credit union leaders use these terms interchangeably, and that is fine for operational conversation. Technically, NIM is a specific calculation: (Interest Income - Interest Expense) / Average Earning Assets. "Spread" can refer to the same thing or to the gap between a specific loan rate and a specific funding rate (for example, a 6.25% auto rate funded by a 4.00% CD has a 225-basis-point spread). NIM gives you the whole-portfolio picture. Spread can describe individual transactions.

How does the Fed funds rate affect net interest margin?

When the Federal Reserve changes the federal funds rate, it does not affect both sides of NIM equally or simultaneously. Deposit costs -- especially on money markets, CDs, and share certificates -- tend to adjust relatively quickly because members and competitors respond to rate signals. Loan yields adjust more slowly because a large portion of the portfolio consists of fixed-rate assets that do not reprice until maturity. This asymmetry is why sudden rate hikes tend to compress NIM in the short term and why daily monitoring is essential during rate-change periods.

What causes net interest margin to decrease?

The three most common causes of NIM compression are: (1) rising deposit costs outpacing loan yield increases -- the classic rate-hike squeeze that hit credit unions hard in 2022-2023; (2) yield curve flattening or inversion that narrows the spread between short-term funding costs and long-term earning rates; and (3) declining loan-to-asset ratios, where high-yielding loan assets run off and get replaced by lower-yielding investment securities. At a $450M credit union, a 10-basis-point compression in NIM represents $450,000 in annualized margin loss.
