NCUA Supervisory Priorities 2026: Key Takeaways

What the 2026 NCUA supervisory priorities mean for your credit union. Actionable guidance on balance sheet risk, IRR, cybersecurity, and compliance.

NCUA Supervisory Priorities 2026: What Credit Union Leaders Need to Know

Every January, the NCUA publishes a letter that tells you exactly where examiners will focus their attention for the year. Most credit union leaders skim it, file it, and move on. That is a mistake.

The supervisory priorities letter is not regulatory boilerplate. It is a roadmap. It tells you which areas of your credit union will receive the deepest scrutiny, which documentation examiners will request first, and which questions your team needs to be ready to answer. If you read it carefully and act on it, you walk into your exam prepared. If you ignore it, you are surprised by questions you could have anticipated.

Here is what the 2026 supervisory priorities actually mean for your credit union — and what to do about each one before your examiner arrives.

The Big Picture: What NCUA Is Telling You

The 2026 supervisory priorities letter carries two themes that credit union leaders need to internalize.

First, the "No Regulation by Enforcement" stance. NCUA is signaling that examination standards will be based on published rules and guidance — examiners should not surprise you with requirements that were not previously communicated. This is good news for credit unions that stay current on regulatory guidance. It does not mean exams will be easier. It means the goalposts will be visible.

Second, the continued emphasis on safety and soundness in a challenging economic environment. Loan delinquency and loss rates are at their highest levels in over a decade. The post-pandemic rate environment has stressed balance sheets. Credit unions that took on aggressive growth strategies in 2022 and 2023 are now seeing the consequences in their portfolios. Examiners know this, and they will be looking hard at whether your credit union is managing these pressures with discipline and documentation — or hoping the numbers get better on their own.

The broader context includes the NCUA Deregulation Project, aimed at reducing unnecessary regulatory burden while preserving the safety-and-soundness framework. This does not change examination rigor — it streamlines the regulatory requirements credit unions must comply with. Prepare for the same depth of examination, applied against a clearer set of expectations.

Balance Sheet Management and Lending

This is the headline priority for 2026, and for good reason. The credit union industry is facing the most challenging credit quality environment since the Great Recession.

What examiners will focus on:

  • Credit administration and underwriting. Examiners will review your underwriting standards against your actual origination data. If your policy says maximum DTI is 43% but your exception reports show 30% of recent originations above that threshold, expect questions — and expect them to dig deeper.
  • Loan performance trends. Delinquency rates, charge-off trends, early payment defaults, and migration analysis. Examiners want to see that you are tracking portfolio deterioration at a granular level, not just in aggregate ratios.
  • ACL methodology. Your Allowance for Credit Losses methodology needs to match your actual portfolio risk. If you adopted CECL with a baseline methodology and have not updated your assumptions since 2023, that is a finding waiting to happen. Examiners will compare your ACL levels to peer benchmarks and your own historical loss experience.
  • Loss mitigation and collections. What are you doing when loans go delinquent? Examiners want to see documented processes: workout programs, modification policies, and evidence that your collection efforts are timely and consistent.
  • Charge-off practices. Are you charging off loans at the right time, per NCUA guidance? Delayed charge-offs inflate your asset quality metrics and mask the real risk in your portfolio.

What to do now:

Pull your underwriting exception report for the last 12 months. If your exception rate exceeds 15%, document the rationale for each exception pattern and what you are doing about it. Review your ACL model assumptions against current economic conditions — if the assumptions were last updated more than 12 months ago, update them before the exam. Review your charge-off timing against NCUA Letter to Credit Unions on charge-off practices. Document everything.

Interest Rate Risk and Liquidity

IRR and liquidity management remain top priorities as credit unions continue adjusting to an interest rate environment that looks nothing like 2019-2021.

What examiners will focus on:

  • IRR modeling practices. Are your model assumptions reasonable and documented? Examiners are looking at non-maturity deposit assumptions in particular — if your model assumes 80% of savings accounts will stay at current rates in a +300 basis point shock, you need strong justification for that assumption.
  • Back-testing. Did your model predictions from 12-18 months ago match what actually happened? If your NEV model predicted a 5% decline and the actual decline was 15%, examiners want to see that you identified the discrepancy and adjusted your assumptions.
  • Stress scenarios. Examiners expect appropriately tiered scenarios — not just +/-100, 200, 300 — but scenarios that reflect your credit union's specific risk profile. If you have heavy CD concentration, your scenarios should stress deposit pricing pressure specifically.
  • Liquidity monitoring. How often do you review your liquidity position? Monthly is the minimum. The credit unions that demonstrate the strongest management are tracking key liquidity indicators weekly or daily. Examiners will review your contingency funding plan and whether it has been tested.
  • ALCO documentation. ALCO meeting minutes are one of the first things examiners request. They should document the scenarios reviewed, the assumptions discussed, the decisions made, and the risk tolerance affirmed. "ALCO met and reviewed reports" is not sufficient documentation.

What to do now:

Validate your IRR model assumptions against actual experience from the last 12 months. Document the results, including any discrepancies and assumption adjustments. Review your contingency funding plan — when was it last tested? Update it if your funding sources or concentration profiles have changed. Ensure your ALCO minutes document decisions, not just discussions.

Operational Risk: Fraud, Payments, and Cybersecurity

Operational risk continues to expand as the threat landscape evolves. Examiners are paying attention to three areas in particular.

Cybersecurity governance. Examiners expect board-level awareness of cybersecurity risks. Has your board received cybersecurity training in the last 12 months? Can board members articulate the credit union's cyber risk profile at a governance level? If the answer is no, address this before your exam.

IT risk assessment and vulnerability management. Your IT risk assessment should be current (updated within the last 12 months) and should reflect your actual technology environment — cloud services, remote access infrastructure, third-party integrations, and any new technology deployments. Vulnerability scans and penetration testing results should be documented with remediation timelines for identified issues.

Payment system resilience and fraud prevention. As payment channels expand (P2P, real-time payments, digital wallets), examiners want to see that your fraud monitoring has kept pace. This includes transaction monitoring, member authentication, and incident response procedures specific to payment fraud.

Vendor management. If you rely on third-party providers for core processing, digital banking, or cybersecurity — and you do — your vendor management program needs to demonstrate ongoing oversight, not just the initial due diligence. Annual vendor reviews, SLA monitoring, and documented assessment of each critical vendor's financial stability and security posture.

AI governance. This is an emerging area for 2026. If your credit union uses AI tools — for lending decisions, member service, fraud detection, or internal operations — examiners are beginning to ask about governance frameworks. Who oversees AI use? How are AI-driven decisions validated? What are the risks, and how are they monitored? This area is still developing, but credit unions that get ahead of it will be in a stronger position.

What to do now:

Schedule board cybersecurity training if it has not happened in the last 12 months. Update your IT risk assessment. Review your incident response plan — run a tabletop exercise if you have not done one recently. If you use AI tools, begin documenting a governance framework even if examiners have not specifically asked for it yet. Being proactive here demonstrates the kind of forward-thinking management examiners value.

Compliance Risk: BSA/AML and Consumer Protection

Compliance risk remains a steady presence in the supervisory priorities. The emphasis for 2026 is on risk-based program design and consumer protection.

BSA/AML expectations. Examiners want to see that your BSA program is tailored to your credit union's specific risk profile — your membership base, your products and services, your geographic footprint. A $150M credit union serving a single county has a different BSA risk profile than a $1.2B credit union with a diverse membership across multiple states. Your BSA risk assessment should reflect your reality, not a template. If your membership demographics or service offerings have changed materially, your risk assessment needs updating.

Consumer financial protection. Three areas will receive particular attention in 2026: overdraft programs (fee structures, opt-in disclosures, and impact on vulnerable members), fair lending practices (with emphasis on HMDA data accuracy and fair lending risk assessments), and Military Lending Act compliance for credit unions serving military communities.

What to do now:

Review your BSA risk assessment — when was it last updated? If your membership or product mix has changed, update it. Audit your overdraft program disclosures for compliance with current guidance. Pull your HMDA data for accuracy checks before submission. If you serve military members, verify MLA compliance across all applicable products.

What Changed From 2025 to 2026

Several shifts are worth noting for credit unions comparing year-over-year priorities.

Loan quality has escalated. While asset quality was a priority in 2025, the 2026 letter reflects a sharper tone on credit deterioration. Delinquency and loss rates at their highest in over a decade means examiners will spend more time in your loan files, not less. If your credit quality is strong, document why — the comparison to peers will be part of the conversation.

The deregulation context is new. The NCUA Deregulation Project adds a layer of nuance. Credit unions may see some regulatory relief in 2026, but the examination process itself remains rigorous. Do not confuse deregulation with reduced expectations — the "No Regulation by Enforcement" stance sets clearer rules, but examiners will still enforce every one of them.

AI oversight is emerging. This was barely mentioned in prior years. Its inclusion in 2026, even at an early stage, signals that AI governance will become a formal examination area in the near future. Early movers who document AI governance frameworks now will be ahead of the curve.

Cybersecurity expectations continue to evolve. The bar rises every year. What constituted an adequate cybersecurity program in 2023 may not meet 2026 expectations. Board training, incident response testing, and vendor oversight are table stakes — examiners are now looking at maturity and continuous improvement.

The Daily Discipline That Covers Every Priority

Here is something most analysis of the supervisory priorities misses: every single priority area benefits from the same three management practices.

Daily financial visibility. Balance sheet management, IRR monitoring, and earnings sustainability all require current data. You cannot demonstrate sound IRR management if you only review your position monthly. You cannot show that you are monitoring credit deterioration if your delinquency data is 30 days old. A daily bottom line — the spread between money bought and money sold, updated every 24 hours — gives examiners evidence that you are watching your financial position in real time.

Documented decisions. Every priority area generates decisions. How did you respond to deposit runoff? Why did you adjust your underwriting standards? What prompted the change to your investment strategy? Examiners evaluate these decisions and the rationale behind them. When decisions are documented with the data that informed them, the expected outcome, and the actual result, examiners see a credit union that operates with discipline — not one that reacts and hopes.

Diagnostic scorecards. A drivers-and-roadblocks scorecard shows why your numbers moved. It is the difference between telling an examiner "our NIM compressed 15 basis points" and telling them "our NIM compressed 15 basis points because cost of funds increased 22 basis points driven by $8M in CD growth at 4.50%, partially offset by a 7 basis point improvement in loan yield from repricing our indirect auto portfolio." The first invites follow-up questions. The second demonstrates mastery.

Your Action Plan: What to Do This Quarter

Here is a prioritized checklist for each supervisory priority area. Do the high-priority items before your next exam. The rest should be addressed within 90 days.

Balance sheet and lending (high priority):

  • Pull your underwriting exception report for the last 12 months and document patterns
  • Review and update your ACL methodology assumptions
  • Audit charge-off timing against NCUA guidance
  • Document your loss mitigation and collections process

Interest rate risk and liquidity (high priority):

  • Back-test your IRR model assumptions against actual results from the last 12 months
  • Update your contingency funding plan if funding sources or concentrations have changed
  • Review ALCO minutes for decision documentation, not just discussion summaries
  • Validate non-maturity deposit assumptions in your IRR model

Operational risk (address within 60 days):

  • Confirm board cybersecurity training is current (within last 12 months)
  • Update your IT risk assessment to reflect current technology environment
  • Run an incident response tabletop exercise
  • Review vendor management for critical third-party providers
  • Begin documenting AI governance if applicable

Compliance risk (address within 90 days):

  • Update your BSA risk assessment if membership or services have changed
  • Audit overdraft program disclosures
  • Review HMDA data for accuracy
  • Verify MLA compliance if serving military members

Build the daily discipline (start now):

  • Track your daily bottom line — even manually — to establish the habit
  • Document every strategic decision with data, rationale, owner, and review date
  • Implement a 2-week decision loop to create accountability and forward motion

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the NCUA supervisory priorities for 2026?

The NCUA's 2026 supervisory priorities focus on five key areas: balance sheet management and lending (with particular attention to rising delinquency rates and ACL adequacy), interest rate risk and liquidity management, operational risk including cybersecurity, fraud prevention, and payment system resilience, compliance risk covering BSA/AML and consumer financial protection, and evolving areas including AI governance. The 2026 letter also emphasizes the "No Regulation by Enforcement" stance, signaling a more collaborative examination approach.

What does "No Regulation by Enforcement" mean for credit unions?

The "No Regulation by Enforcement" policy means NCUA intends to set clear expectations through published rules and guidance rather than imposing new requirements through the examination process. For credit unions, this means examiners should not introduce surprise standards during an exam that were not previously communicated. However, this does not reduce the rigor of examinations — it means the rules are published upfront so credit unions can prepare. Examiners will still enforce existing regulations and safety-and-soundness standards thoroughly.

How should credit unions prepare for NCUA exam priorities?

Prepare by reviewing each priority area against your current practices. For balance sheet management: audit your ACL methodology and underwriting standards. For IRR: validate your model assumptions and document ALCO decisions. For cybersecurity: update your incident response plan and confirm board cybersecurity training is current. For compliance: review your BSA risk assessment and overdraft disclosures. Most importantly, build a daily discipline of monitoring your financial position and documenting your decisions — this addresses multiple priorities simultaneously.

What changed in NCUA priorities from 2025 to 2026?

Key shifts from 2025 to 2026 include: increased emphasis on loan quality deterioration as delinquency and loss rates hit their highest levels in over a decade, the formalization of the "No Regulation by Enforcement" stance, expanded attention to AI governance and oversight as more credit unions adopt AI tools, continued focus on cybersecurity with evolving expectations around incident response and board training, and the broader context of the NCUA Deregulation Project aimed at reducing regulatory burden while maintaining safety and soundness.

How do NCUA supervisory priorities affect my credit union?

Supervisory priorities directly influence what examiners spend their time on during your exam. If balance sheet management is a top priority and your credit union has rising delinquencies, expect deep scrutiny of your lending practices, ACL methodology, and loss mitigation programs. Priorities do not create new regulations — they tell you where examiners will focus their attention and ask the toughest questions. Use them as a roadmap to strengthen your documentation and practices in the areas most likely to receive examination emphasis.
