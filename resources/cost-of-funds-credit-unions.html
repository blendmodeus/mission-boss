Cost of Funds Explained for Credit Union CFOs

What cost of funds means for credit unions, how to calculate it, what drives it, and five strategies to manage it. Includes NCUA 2025 data and worked examples.

Cost of Funds Explained: What Every Credit Union CFO Needs to Know

Here is a scenario I have seen at least fifty times. A credit union CFO pulls up the monthly financials, sees that net interest margin compressed 12 basis points, and the first question from the CEO is: "What happened?" The answer, almost always, starts with the same three words: cost of funds.

Your cost of funds is the rate you are paying to acquire the money you lend out and invest. It is half the NIM equation -- the half that has been hardest to manage since the Fed started moving rates in 2022. And yet at most credit unions, the cost of funds number shows up once a month, buried on page seven of the financials, with no breakdown of what moved it.

This article gives you the full picture. What cost of funds is, how to calculate it, what makes it go up and down, where credit unions stand right now, and what you can actually do about it.

What Is Cost of Funds?

Cost of funds is the average rate a financial institution pays for the money it uses to fund its lending and investment activities. Think of it as the wholesale cost of your raw material. A manufacturer pays for steel. A credit union pays for money. The rate you pay for that money is your cost of funds.

In credit union terms, cost of funds covers:

  • Share dividends you pay on regular savings, checking, and money market accounts
  • Certificate (CD) interest you pay on termed deposits
  • Borrowing costs on FHLB advances, fed funds purchased, and other wholesale funding
  • Any other interest-bearing liability that funds your balance sheet

The lower your cost of funds, the more spread you keep between what you pay and what you earn. That spread is your net interest margin -- the number that determines whether your credit union can cover operating expenses, build reserves, and invest in member services.

Cost of funds vs. cost of capital: These get confused regularly, so let's separate them. Cost of funds refers specifically to the interest expense on deposits and borrowings -- the liabilities side of the balance sheet. Cost of capital is a broader concept that includes the required return on equity (net worth). A credit union's cost of capital would factor in the opportunity cost of retained earnings and the regulatory requirement to maintain a certain net worth ratio. When your NCUA examiner asks about cost of funds, they are asking about deposit and borrowing costs. When a finance textbook mentions cost of capital, it is a different animal.

How to Calculate Cost of Funds

The basic formula:

Cost of Funds = Total Interest Expense / Average Interest-Bearing Liabilities

This gives you the blended rate you are paying across all funding sources.

Worked example -- a $400M credit union:

Funding Source Average Balance Interest Paid Rate
Regular shares & savings $95M $0.29M 0.30%
Money market accounts $72M $2.16M 3.00%
Share certificates (CDs) $108M $4.48M 4.15%
IRA accounts $18M $0.58M 3.22%
FHLB advances $35M $1.72M 4.91%
Total $328M $9.23M

Cost of Funds = $9.23M / $328M = 2.81%

That 2.81% is the blended cost -- the weighted average rate this credit union is paying across every dollar of funded liabilities. It is the number that, subtracted from the yield on earning assets, determines the spread.

Blended cost of funds vs. marginal cost of funds:

The blended number above tells you what you are paying on the money you already have. The marginal cost of funds tells you what it costs to bring in the next dollar. These are different questions, and both matter.

If this credit union needs $5M in new deposits tomorrow and the only way to attract them is a 4.50% CD special, the marginal cost of those new funds is 4.50% -- well above the 2.81% blended rate. That $5M at 4.50% raises the overall blended cost, but more importantly, it sets the floor for what new lending needs to earn. If you are originating auto loans at 6.25%, the spread on that new money is only 175 basis points. On the existing book at 2.81%, the same auto loan earns 344 basis points of spread.

Every time a CFO sets a deposit rate, they are making a marginal cost-of-funds decision. The blended number tells you where you are. The marginal number tells you where you are heading.

What Makes Up a Credit Union's Cost of Funds

Not all money costs the same. The funding mix -- where your dollars come from -- is one of the biggest levers you have.

Regular Share and Savings Accounts

This is historically the cheapest money in the building. At many credit unions, regular shares still carry a dividend rate between 0.10% and 0.50%. Members keep these balances for convenience and access, not for yield. A $400M credit union might fund $95M of its balance sheet at an average cost of 0.30%.

The risk: taking this money for granted. When rate-sensitive members see a 4.50% money market at the bank down the street, some of that cheap money walks. I have watched credit unions lose $15M to $20M in regular share balances over a six-month period during rate-hike cycles -- money that cost 0.25% that gets replaced by FHLB advances at 4.90%. That swap alone can move cost of funds by 15 to 20 basis points on a $400M balance sheet.

Share Certificates (CDs)

Certificates are the double-edged sword of credit union funding. They provide certainty -- you know the rate and the term. But they also lock you in. A credit union that ran a 4.50% 18-month CD special in mid-2024 is still paying that rate today even though the market has shifted.

The current situation: NCUA data shows roughly 25% of termed shares and borrowings across the industry are maturing within the next year. That is a repricing opportunity. If those CDs were originated at 4.25% to 4.75% and can be renewed at 3.75% to 4.00%, the cost-of-funds impact on a $400M credit union with $108M in certificates could be 20 to 40 basis points -- $650,000 to $1.3M in annualized interest expense savings.

But it only works if you know which CDs are maturing, when, and at what rates. And if you are tracking that monthly, you are seeing the repricing opportunity after half of it has already passed.

Money Market Accounts

Money market balances are rate-sensitive by nature. The members who hold them are watching what competitors offer. They are the first to move when rates change.

The challenge for CFOs: money market rates move with the market, but they move unevenly. When rates rise, members demand competitive yields quickly. When rates fall, members are slow to accept lower rates. This asymmetry means money market cost of funds tends to ratchet upward and only grudgingly moves down. A credit union paying 3.50% on money markets when the competitive market has eased to 3.00% is overpaying by 50 basis points on every dollar in that bucket. On $72M in money market balances, that is $360,000 a year in unnecessary interest expense.

Borrowed Funds (FHLB Advances, Fed Funds Purchased)

Borrowed funds are typically the most expensive money on a credit union's balance sheet. FHLB advances might carry rates of 4.50% to 5.25% depending on term and current market conditions.

The industry trend is clear: credit unions have been working to reduce their reliance on borrowings. NCUA 2025 data shows borrowings declined from 6.0% to 5.2% of total assets across the industry. That reduction reflects credit unions replacing expensive wholesale funding with deposit growth or simply shrinking balance sheets to reduce cost. On a $400M credit union, reducing borrowings from 6.0% of assets ($24M) to 5.2% ($20.8M) at a 4.91% rate saves roughly $157,000 in annual interest expense.

Non-Member Deposits and Other Sources

Some credit unions accept non-member deposits (where charter and regulation allow), use brokered deposits, or tap other liability sources. These tend to be rate-driven, which means they come at market cost and leave when a better rate appears elsewhere. They add volume but not stability. Factor their cost in, but do not build a funding strategy around them.

Cost of Funds Trends: Where Credit Unions Stand Now

The story of cost of funds over the past three years has been a slow-motion squeeze followed by the beginning of relief.

2022-2023: The Fed's rate-hike cycle pushed short-term rates from near zero to over 5%. Credit unions that relied on wholesale funding saw those costs spike immediately. Deposit costs lagged but climbed steadily as members demanded competitive rates and competitors launched aggressive CD specials. Cost of funds across the industry roughly doubled over this period.

2024: The squeeze peaked. Deposit costs continued rising even after the Fed paused hikes because of locked-in CD rates and competitive pressure. Many credit unions saw their cost of funds reach levels not experienced in over 15 years. NIM compressed industry-wide, from historical norms above 3.25% down toward 3.00%.

2025: Relief began. NCUA data shows industry NIM recovered to 3.39% of average assets, up 39 basis points from the prior year. Borrowings declined as credit unions replaced expensive wholesale funding. But the recovery was uneven -- credit unions that had loaded up on long-term, high-rate CDs in 2023 and 2024 are still carrying those costs. The lag effect is real: it takes 12 to 18 months for a CD book to fully reprice after rates shift.

The lag effect explained: When the Fed cuts rates, your newest deposit and borrowing rates drop relatively quickly. But certificates originated six or twelve months ago at peak rates are still on the books, still paying 4.25% or 4.50%, and will not reprice until they mature. If 25% of your termed deposits mature this year and 75% do not, three-quarters of your certificate book is locked at yesterday's rates. Your blended cost of funds moves slowly even when the market moves fast. Credit unions that are tracking this monthly discover the lag. Credit unions that track it daily can manage around it.

The Relationship Between Cost of Funds and Net Interest Margin

Cost of funds is one side of the NIM equation. Earning asset yield is the other. NIM is the gap between them.

NIM = Earning Asset Yield - Cost of Funds (simplified)

When cost of funds rises faster than earning asset yield, NIM compresses. When cost of funds drops faster than asset yields, NIM expands. This is the fundamental dynamic that every credit union CFO is managing, whether they frame it that way or not.

The 2022-2024 cycle illustrated this perfectly. The Fed raised rates, and credit unions' earning asset yields rose -- but slowly, because a significant portion of the loan book was in fixed-rate mortgages and auto loans that do not reprice. Meanwhile, deposit costs rose quickly because maturing CDs repriced at higher rates and members moved money into higher-yielding accounts. The result: cost of funds outpaced asset yields, and NIM compressed.

At a $500M credit union, the math is stark. If earning asset yield is 4.85% and cost of funds rises from 2.50% to 3.00%, NIM drops from 2.35% to 1.85% -- a 50-basis-point compression that translates to $2.5M in lost annual net interest income. That is not a hypothetical. That pattern played out at credit unions across the country.

The path back to healthy NIM runs through cost of funds. You can push earning asset yields higher by adjusting loan pricing and shifting the investment portfolio, but those moves take time. Repricing deposits and reducing borrowing costs can deliver faster results -- if you know exactly what you are paying, on which products, and where the repricing opportunities are. Five Strategies to Manage Cost of Funds

1. Reprice Maturing CDs and Borrowings at Current Market Rates

With 25% of termed shares and borrowings maturing industry-wide in the coming year, repricing is the single biggest near-term lever. If your CD book carries a blended rate of 4.15% and the current market allows renewal at 3.75%, every $10M that reprices saves $40,000 in annual interest expense. On a $108M certificate portfolio, repricing the 25% that matures could save $108,000 to $432,000 depending on the rate differential.

The discipline: know your maturity schedule in detail. Not just the total maturing this quarter, but the specific amounts, rates, and dates. A $3.2M CD maturing on March 15 at 4.50% is a different repricing conversation than a $800,000 CD maturing on March 28 at 3.90%.

2. Shift the Funding Mix Toward Lower-Cost Core Deposits

Every dollar that moves from a 4.91% FHLB advance to a 0.30% regular share account saves 461 basis points. The challenge is that you cannot force members to deposit more. But you can create the conditions: competitive checking products, relationship benefits, direct deposit incentives, and a member experience that keeps primary financial relationships at your credit union.

Credit unions that have deliberately invested in growing core deposits -- not through rate competition, but through relationship depth -- have structurally lower cost of funds. A credit union where 40% of funding comes from regular shares at 0.30% starts every day with a cost-of-funds advantage over one that funds 25% from regular shares and makes up the difference with 4.50% CDs and 4.91% borrowings.

3. Adopt Relationship-Based Pricing That Rewards Full-Wallet Members

Instead of offering the same CD rate to every member, price based on relationship depth. A member with a checking account, direct deposit, a credit card, and an auto loan gets a better CD rate than a single-service member who only parked money for the rate. This approach retains core relationships without overpaying for rate-driven deposits.

The cost-of-funds impact: members who hold multiple products tend to keep larger balances in lower-cost accounts (checking, regular savings) alongside their certificates. The blended cost of a full-relationship member is almost always lower than a rate-chaser who only holds a CD.

4. Reduce Reliance on Rate-Driven, Hot-Money Deposits

Hot money -- deposits that arrived because of a promotional rate and will leave when a better one appears -- is expensive, unstable, and distorts your cost of funds. A $5M deposit that arrived at 4.75% and leaves six months later forces you to replace it, often at whatever the market demands at that moment.

The discipline is tracking where your deposit growth is coming from. If 60% of new CDs in the last quarter came from members who hold no other product at your credit union, you are building a funding base on rate sensitivity. That is a cost-of-funds problem waiting to happen.

5. Track Cost of Funds Daily, Not Monthly

This is the one most credit unions skip, and it is the one that makes the other four strategies possible. You cannot reprice what you cannot see. You cannot evaluate your funding mix shift if the data is 30 days old. You cannot tell whether your relationship pricing strategy is working if you measure it once a month.

When you see your cost of funds every day, you catch repricing opportunities as they emerge, not after they have passed. You see the impact of a deposit promotion within days, not at month-end. You know immediately when a competitor's rate move is pulling money out of your low-cost accounts.

Cost of Funds in NCUA Exams

Your examiner cares about cost of funds. It is central to the interest rate risk assessment and the overall earnings evaluation.

What examiners look for:

  • Funding concentration risk. If more than 30% of your funding comes from one source category (e.g., share certificates or FHLB advances), expect questions. Diversification of funding sources signals prudent management.
  • Rate sensitivity documentation. Can you demonstrate that you understand how your cost of funds will change if rates move up or down 100, 200, or 300 basis points? The standard ALM shock tests address this, but examiners want to see that management actually uses the results.
  • Trend awareness. An examiner who sees your cost of funds climbing for four consecutive quarters will ask what you are doing about it. Having a documented strategy -- not just awareness -- is the difference between a satisfactory finding and a supervisory concern.
  • Decision trail. Did the board approve the CD special that raised cost of funds by 15 basis points? Is there documentation showing the analysis behind that decision? This is where most credit unions fall short -- the decision was made, but the documentation is thin.

Credit unions that track cost of funds daily and document management responses to changes in real time have a natural advantage in exams. When the examiner asks "What drove your cost of funds increase in Q3?", you can point to specific dates, specific events, and specific management actions -- not a monthly summary prepared three weeks after the fact. Key Takeaways

  • Cost of funds is the rate you pay for the money you use to fund lending and investments. It is half the NIM equation and the half that moved most aggressively during the 2022-2025 rate cycle.
  • The formula: Total Interest Expense / Average Interest-Bearing Liabilities. At a $400M credit union with $9.23M in interest expense and $328M in average funded liabilities, cost of funds is 2.81%.
  • Not all money costs the same. Regular shares at 0.30% and FHLB advances at 4.91% are on the same balance sheet but create very different margin dynamics. Your funding mix is a strategic choice.
  • The lag is real. When rates shift, your cost of funds moves slowly because existing CDs and borrowings reprice on their own schedule. With 25% of termed deposits maturing industry-wide, the repricing window is now.
  • Five levers: Reprice maturing CDs, shift toward core deposits, use relationship-based pricing, reduce hot-money reliance, and track cost of funds daily.
  • Examiners care. Funding concentration, rate sensitivity documentation, and a clear decision trail all factor into the interest rate risk assessment.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you calculate cost of funds for a credit union?

Divide total interest expense by average interest-bearing liabilities. For a $400M credit union paying $9.23M in total interest expense on $328M in average funded liabilities, cost of funds is $9.23M / $328M = 2.81%. Include all interest paid on shares, certificates, money markets, IRAs, and borrowed funds. Use actual interest paid, not stated rates, because compounding and promotional adjustments can create differences between the two. For trend analysis, calculate this monthly at minimum -- or daily if your data infrastructure supports it.

What is a good cost of funds ratio for a credit union?

It depends on your market, membership base, and funding strategy. As a rough benchmark, credit unions with strong core deposit bases (heavy in regular shares and checking) often achieve cost of funds between 1.50% and 2.50% in the current environment. Those with heavier reliance on certificates and borrowings may run 2.80% to 3.50% or higher. The absolute number matters less than the trend and the relationship to your earning asset yield. A cost of funds of 2.81% paired with an earning asset yield of 5.50% produces a healthy 269-basis-point spread. The same cost of funds with a 4.50% earning asset yield produces a 169-basis-point spread that may not cover operating expenses.

What is the difference between cost of funds and cost of capital?

Cost of funds refers specifically to the interest expense on deposits and borrowings -- the price a credit union pays for the liabilities that fund its balance sheet. Cost of capital is a broader concept from corporate finance that includes the required return on equity. For a credit union, cost of capital would theoretically include the return needed on retained earnings (net worth) to maintain adequate capitalization. In practice, credit union CFOs use "cost of funds" when discussing deposit and borrowing costs, and the term appears in NCUA exam contexts. "Cost of capital" shows up more in strategic planning and merger analysis. They are related but not interchangeable.

How does the Federal Reserve affect a credit union's cost of funds?

The Fed sets the federal funds rate, which directly influences short-term borrowing costs (FHLB advances, overnight funding) and indirectly affects deposit pricing through market competition. When the Fed raises rates, credit unions face immediate pressure on borrowed funds and gradual pressure on deposits as members seek higher yields. When the Fed cuts, borrowed fund costs drop relatively quickly, but deposit costs fall slowly because existing CDs mature on their own schedule. This asymmetry -- fast up, slow down -- is why cost of funds tends to lag Fed rate movements by 6 to 18 months, and why the 25% of termed shares and borrowings maturing in the coming year represent a significant repricing opportunity for credit unions.

How can a credit union reduce its cost of funds?

Five practical approaches: (1) Reprice maturing CDs and borrowings at current lower market rates rather than auto-renewing at previous terms. (2) Grow low-cost core deposits -- regular shares, checking accounts -- through relationship depth rather than rate competition. (3) Implement relationship-based deposit pricing that rewards full-wallet members instead of offering the best rate to single-service rate chasers. (4) Reduce reliance on wholesale borrowings by replacing FHLB advances with deposit growth where possible. (5) Track cost of funds daily so you can catch repricing opportunities and competitive pressures in real time, not 30 to 45 days after they occur.
