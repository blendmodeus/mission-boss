Cost of Funds Index Explained for Credit Unions

Learn what the Cost of Funds Index (COFI) is, how it is calculated, and why your credit union's internal cost of funds is the real lever on your net interest margin.

Cost of Funds Index Explained: What Credit Union Leaders Need to Know

Most credit union leaders hear "cost of funds index" and think ARM pricing -- adjustable-rate mortgages tied to some external benchmark published by Freddie Mac or the old Federal Home Loan Bank system. That is one definition, and we will cover it here. But if you run a credit union, the cost of funds that actually keeps you up at night is not an index published in a press release. It is the number buried in your own balance sheet: the blended rate you are paying for every dollar of deposits, borrowings, and CD specials that fund your loan portfolio. That number -- your internal cost of funds -- is the single biggest lever on your net interest margin. And most credit unions only see it once a month, 30 to 45 days after the fact.

Let's unpack both versions of cost of funds, starting with the one Google brought you here for.

What Is the Cost of Funds Index?

The Cost of Funds Index (COFI) is a benchmark interest rate that reflects the average interest expense paid by financial institutions on their deposits and borrowings. It is most commonly associated with adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) pricing, where a lender ties the borrower's rate to COFI plus a margin.

The most well-known version was the 11th District Cost of Funds Index, published monthly by the Federal Home Loan Bank of San Francisco. It tracked the cost of funds for savings institutions in Arizona, California, and Nevada from 1981 until its retirement in 2022. If you ever had a COFI-indexed ARM, that was the number driving your rate adjustments.

Today, the primary cost of funds index still in use is the National Cost of Funds Index, published by Freddie Mac. It reflects the weighted average interest cost across a broader set of institutions nationwide.

Who publishes it: Freddie Mac (national); the 11th District version was published by the FHLB of San Francisco until 2022.

Who uses it: Mortgage lenders pricing ARMs, financial analysts benchmarking funding costs, and regulators tracking industry trends.

How the Cost of Funds Index Is Calculated

The COFI calculation is straightforward in concept: take the total interest expense a group of institutions paid on their deposits and borrowings during a period, then divide by the average balance of those funds.

The formula:

COFI = Total Interest Expense / Average Balance of All Interest-Bearing Liabilities

For the old 11th District index, the FHLB of San Francisco collected monthly data from member savings institutions -- interest paid on savings accounts, CDs, checking accounts, and borrowed money -- then computed the weighted average cost. The resulting index moved slowly because it reflected the blended cost of existing deposits, not just new money. A CD opened at 5.25% three years ago still dragged on the average alongside a new money market account paying 4.10%.

The national index works the same way but draws from a wider pool of institutions.

Key characteristic: COFI is a lagging indicator. It reflects what institutions already paid for money, not what they are paying today. That lag is part of why COFI-indexed ARMs historically adjusted more slowly than those tied to the prime rate or Treasury yields.

COFI vs. Other Rate Indexes

Not all rate benchmarks behave the same way. Here is how COFI stacks up against the indexes you see most often:

Index What It Tracks Speed of Movement Common Use
COFI Actual funding costs of financial institutions Slow (lagging) ARM pricing
Prime Rate Rate banks charge their best commercial borrowers Fast (moves with Fed) HELOCs, credit cards
SOFR Overnight secured lending between banks Fast (daily market rate) Replaced LIBOR for commercial lending
1-Year Treasury U.S. government borrowing cost Moderate ARM pricing, investment benchmarks

The practical difference: when the Fed raises rates by 75 basis points, the prime rate moves the next day. SOFR adjusts within the week. COFI might take six months to fully reflect the change because it is dragging along the cost of deposits that were priced months or years earlier.

For credit union leaders, this distinction matters less for mortgage pricing and more for understanding why your own cost of funds behaves the way it does. Your balance sheet has the same lagging characteristic as COFI -- old CDs at low rates rolling off and repricing higher, share accounts adjusting at different speeds, borrowings locked at fixed rates. The question is whether you are seeing that shift in real time or finding out about it six weeks later.

Why Cost of Funds Matters Beyond Mortgage Pricing

Here is where most articles on this topic stop. They explain COFI as a mortgage index and move on. But if you are a credit union CEO or CFO, the external cost of funds index is a footnote. Your internal cost of funds is the story.

Your cost of funds is the blended rate you are paying for all the money that funds your earning assets. At a $400M credit union, that might look like this:

  • Regular shares (40% of funding): paying 0.45%
  • Money market accounts (20%): paying 3.85%
  • CDs (25%): paying 4.50% weighted average
  • Borrowings (10%): paying 5.10%
  • Non-interest bearing (5%): paying 0%

Blend those together and your cost of funds is roughly 2.65%. If your loan portfolio is yielding 5.85%, your gross spread is 3.20%. That spread -- the distance between the rate you bought money at and the rate you sold it for -- is your net interest margin, and it is the clearest signal of your credit union's financial health.

When your cost of funds moves 15 basis points in the wrong direction and you do not catch it for 45 days, the math gets ugly fast. On a $400M balance sheet, 15 basis points is $600,000 in annualized margin compression. That is real money walking out the door while you are looking at last month's numbers.

Cost of Funds and Net Interest Margin: The Connection

The credit union industry posted a net interest margin of 3.39% of average assets in 2025, up from 3.00% a year earlier, according to NCUA data. That improvement -- 39 basis points industry-wide -- was driven almost entirely by cost of funds moving in the right direction as rate-hike pressure on deposits eased and expensive borrowings rolled off. Industry borrowings declined from 6.0% to 5.2% of total assets during that period, putting $80.4 billion in net interest income on the books.

The credit unions that captured the most margin improvement were the ones that saw the shift early. They repriced maturing CDs aggressively. They paid down FHLB advances as they came due. They moved relationship-priced deposits into tiers that rewarded loyalty without overpaying.

The credit unions that missed it? They were reviewing 30-to-45-day-old call report data, having board conversations about numbers that were already stale, and making deposit pricing decisions based on what competitors posted last month rather than what their own balance sheet needed today.

How Credit Unions Can Manage Their Cost of Funds

Managing cost of funds is not a one-time project. It is a daily discipline. Here are the levers that matter most:

Reprice maturing deposits strategically. When a $12M batch of 18-month CDs matures at 4.75%, you have a window. Do you renew at 4.25%? Drop to 3.90% and accept some runoff? The right answer depends on your current funding position -- which means you need current data, not last month's.

Pay down expensive borrowings. If you are carrying FHLB advances at 5.10% and your deposit growth can replace that funding at 3.50%, every dollar you shift saves 160 basis points. Industry-wide, credit unions reduced borrowings from 6.0% to 5.2% of assets in 2025. The ones that moved fastest captured the most margin.

Adopt relationship-based deposit pricing. Paying 4.50% on a CD to a member who also has a checking account, auto loan, and credit card is different than paying 4.50% to a rate shopper who will leave in six months. Price accordingly.

Reduce the data lag. This is the one most credit unions have not addressed. You can execute every deposit strategy perfectly and still lose ground if you are finding out about cost shifts 30 to 45 days late. The gap between when your cost of funds moves and when you see it is where margin goes to die.

Key Takeaways

  • The Cost of Funds Index (COFI) is a benchmark reflecting the average interest expense financial institutions pay on deposits and borrowings. The 11th District version retired in 2022; the national index published by Freddie Mac remains active.
  • COFI is a lagging indicator, making it slower-moving than prime rate, SOFR, or Treasury-based benchmarks.
  • For credit union leaders, the external COFI matters far less than your internal cost of funds -- the blended rate you pay for all funding sources.
  • Your cost of funds is the single biggest lever on your net interest margin. A 15-basis-point shift on a $400M balance sheet equals $600,000 in annualized margin impact.
  • Managing cost of funds requires daily visibility, not monthly reviews of stale data.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the current cost of funds index rate?

The national Cost of Funds Index rate fluctuates monthly based on data collected by Freddie Mac. As of early 2026, the index reflects the declining rate environment following the 2022-2023 hiking cycle. Check Freddie Mac's published data for the most current figure, but remember -- the external COFI matters far less to your credit union than your own internal cost of funds, which you should be tracking daily.

What is the difference between COFI and the prime rate?

COFI reflects the actual average cost financial institutions pay on their existing deposits and borrowings -- it is backward-looking and slow-moving. The prime rate is the rate banks charge their best commercial borrowers and moves almost immediately when the Federal Reserve changes the federal funds rate. A 50-basis-point Fed hike will show up in the prime rate the next day but might take months to fully flow through COFI.

How do credit unions calculate their internal cost of funds?

Take your total interest expense for a period -- everything you paid on share accounts, CDs, money markets, and borrowings -- and divide it by your average interest-bearing liabilities for that same period. The result is your blended cost of funds rate. At a $350M credit union paying $8.75M annually in interest expense on $330M in average interest-bearing liabilities, your cost of funds is approximately 2.65%.

What replaced the 11th District COFI?

The 11th District Cost of Funds Index, published by the Federal Home Loan Bank of San Francisco, was retired in January 2022 after more than 40 years. Existing ARM contracts tied to the 11th District COFI transitioned to replacement indexes, most commonly the Freddie Mac national COFI or SOFR-based alternatives. The national COFI continues to be published and functions as the primary cost of funds benchmark.

Why does cost of funds matter more than interest rates for credit unions?

Interest rates set the environment, but your cost of funds determines your margin. Two credit unions operating in the same rate environment can have wildly different costs of funds based on their deposit mix, CD pricing, and borrowing levels. A credit union heavy in CDs at 4.75% and FHLB advances at 5.10% will have a fundamentally different margin profile than one funded primarily by relationship checking and savings accounts at 1.20%. The rate environment is the weather. Your cost of funds is how your house is built.
