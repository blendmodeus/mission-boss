Interest Rate Risk Management for Credit Unions: Strategies That Work

If your ALCO is reviewing interest rate risk once a quarter and calling it managed, you are not managing it. You are documenting your exposure after the damage is done.

I have spent 25 years sitting in the rooms where rate risk either gets handled or gets ignored. The pattern is always the same: a credit union runs its IRR model, reviews the results at the next ALCO meeting, files the report, and moves on. Then rates move. The spread compresses. Earnings take a hit. And six weeks later, the leadership team discovers what happened -- too late to do anything except explain it to the board and the examiner.

Interest rate risk management is not a reporting exercise. It is the daily discipline of understanding how rate movements affect the spread between the money you buy and the money you sell -- and having the framework to respond before the damage is baked into your financials.

This guide covers what credit union leaders between $100M and $2B in assets actually need to build an IRR program that works -- not just one that passes exam.

What Interest Rate Risk Means for Your Credit Union

Interest rate risk is the exposure your credit union faces when changes in market interest rates affect your earnings, your capital, or both. It is the "S" in the CAMELS rating -- Sensitivity to Market Risk -- and it has been a top NCUA examination focus since the Fed started moving aggressively in 2022.

Strip it down to the essentials: you buy money from members at a certain rate (deposits, borrowings) and you sell money at a higher rate (loans, investments). The gap between those rates is your spread. When rates move, the timing and magnitude of repricing on each side of the balance sheet rarely match up. That mismatch is interest rate risk.

For a $500M credit union, a 50 basis point compression in net interest margin translates to roughly $2.5M in lost annual income. That is not abstract. That is four positions you cannot fill, a branch renovation you have to delay, and a strategic plan that stalls.

Interest rate risk management sits at the center of your overall asset liability management program. It is the engine that drives most ALM decisions -- from loan pricing to deposit strategy to investment portfolio positioning.

The Four Types of Interest Rate Risk

NCUA expects your IRR program to address all four types of interest rate risk. Each one hits your balance sheet differently, and each requires a different monitoring approach.

1. Repricing Risk

Repricing risk is the most straightforward. It arises when your assets and liabilities mature or reprice at different times. A credit union with $150M in 30-year fixed-rate mortgages funded by $150M in 12-month share certificates has significant repricing risk. When those CDs mature and members demand 75 basis points more, your cost of funds jumps while your asset yield stays locked for decades.

A $300M credit union I worked with had 62% of its loan portfolio in fixed-rate products with an average remaining maturity of 8.4 years, while 71% of its deposits repriced within 12 months. When the Fed moved 225 basis points in a single cycle, that timing mismatch cost them $1.9M in annualized spread compression.

2. Basis Risk

Basis risk occurs when rates that are supposed to move together move at different speeds. Your prime-linked home equity lines and your cost of funds are both tied to "market rates," but they do not reprice in lockstep.

Between 2022 and 2024, many credit unions watched deposit costs climb 180 basis points while their prime-linked asset yields rose only 125 basis points in effective yield terms. The Fed funds rate moved, but the basis between asset and liability repricing created a spread compression that standard parallel rate shock models underestimated.

This is the type of risk that quarterly reports miss most often. Basis risk plays out in daily spread movements -- and if you are not watching the spread daily, you will not see it until the quarterly model confirms what already happened.

3. Yield Curve Risk

Yield curve risk shows up when the shape of the yield curve changes. Credit unions that borrow short and lend long depend on a normal upward-sloping curve to generate spread. When the curve flattens or inverts -- as it did from mid-2022 through early 2024 -- that fundamental business model gets squeezed.

Your IRR model should be testing non-parallel shifts: curve flattening, steepening, and inversion scenarios. If your vendor only runs parallel rate shocks (rates up 200, rates down 200), you are missing one of the most practically impactful risk types. And your examiner knows it.

4. Options Risk

Options risk is the one credit unions underestimate most. Members hold embedded options in your products: the option to prepay a mortgage, withdraw a CD early, or shift funds from savings to a money market account chasing yield.

When rates dropped in 2020-2021, mortgage prepayment speeds surged and credit unions saw their highest-yielding assets disappear as members refinanced. When rates rose in 2022-2023, members broke CDs despite early withdrawal penalties to chase higher rates elsewhere. Both scenarios changed the cash flow characteristics of the balance sheet in ways that were hard to predict.

A $200M credit union saw $8.2M in unexpected mortgage prepayments in a single quarter when rates dipped briefly in late 2024 -- payoffs that their model had not projected because the prepayment assumptions were calibrated to the rising-rate environment. That is options risk in action.

How to Measure IRR: Three Methods Compared

Your IRR program should use multiple measurement methods. Each one tells you something different. NCUA expects both short-term and long-term measures for a complete picture.

GAP Analysis

GAP analysis is the simplest measure. It buckets your assets and liabilities by repricing period and measures the mismatch. If you have $120M repricing in the next year on the asset side and $170M on the liability side, you are liability-sensitive by $50M. Rising rates will likely hurt you because your costs reprice faster than your revenue.

GAP is useful as a quick directional indicator. It is easy for board members to understand and gives you a headline number to track over time. But it ignores optionality, assumes everything reprices on schedule, and tells you nothing about the dollar impact on earnings.

NII Simulation

Net Interest Income simulation models your projected earnings under different rate scenarios -- typically shocks of +/-100, 200, and 300 basis points over 12 to 24 months. A well-built NII model tells you that a +200 basis point shock might reduce your net interest income by $1.4M next year. That is a dollar amount your ALCO can act on.

NII simulation is the workhorse of tactical IRR management. It drives pricing decisions, product mix adjustments, and balance sheet positioning. But it is only as good as the assumptions behind it -- and most credit unions do not challenge their assumptions nearly enough.

NEV Analysis

Net Economic Value calculates the present value of all assets minus all liabilities under different rate scenarios. If your NEV drops 32% under a +300 basis point shock, your balance sheet has significant embedded long-term risk, even if your near-term NII looks stable.

NEV captures structural risk that NII simulation misses. A credit union can show stable projected NII for the next year while sitting on an NEV position that signals serious problems in years two through five.

Method Measures Horizon Best For
GAP Analysis Repricing mismatch Point-in-time Directional read
NII Simulation Earnings at risk 12-24 months Tactical decisions
NEV Economic value at risk Full balance sheet life Long-term structural risk

Use all three. Your examiner expects it, and your balance sheet management requires it.

What NCUA Expects From Your IRR Program

NCUA's interest rate risk supervisory framework is built on four pillars: identify, measure, monitor, and control. Under 12 CFR Part 741, Appendix A, every federally insured credit union must have a comprehensive IRR management program. Here is what that means in practice.

Board-Approved IRR Policy

Your IRR policy must include clearly defined risk tolerance limits (for example: NII decline under a +300 basis point shock will not exceed 15% from base case, NEV decline will not exceed 25%), approved measurement methodologies, reporting requirements, roles and responsibilities, and remediation procedures for when limits are breached.

The policy must be reviewed and approved by the board at least annually. If your policy has not been updated since 2023, it does not reflect the current rate environment, and your examiner will note it.

ALCO Oversight

NCUA expects an active ALCO or equivalent committee that meets at least monthly, reviews current IRR metrics, discusses market conditions, makes documented decisions, and tracks the execution of prior decisions. The key word is "active." Meeting minutes that show a review-and-file pattern -- data presented, no discussion, no decisions -- are a red flag.

Model Assumptions and Back-Testing

Your examiner will scrutinize the assumptions in your IRR model: deposit decay rates, prepayment speeds, non-maturity deposit behavior, pricing betas, and new business assumptions. These assumptions must be documented, justified, and periodically validated against actual outcomes.

Back-testing is where most mid-size credit unions fall short. You should be comparing what your model predicted 12 months ago against what actually happened. If the model projected stable NII under a +100 basis point scenario and your actual NII dropped $800K, something is wrong with the assumptions. Document the analysis and the adjustments you make.

Common Exam Findings

The findings I see most often at credit unions between $100M and $2B:

  1. Stale assumptions. Deposit decay rates from 2019 do not reflect 2026 member behavior. Update them.
  2. Insufficient scenario coverage. Parallel shocks only. No yield curve scenarios, no basis risk analysis.
  3. No connection between results and action. The model shows risk, the ALCO reviews it, nothing changes. Examiners look for evidence that IRR measurement drives decisions.
  4. Outdated policies. Risk limits that were set in a zero-rate environment and never recalibrated.
  5. Weak documentation. ALCO minutes that read like a summary, not a decision log.

Building Your IRR Management Framework

A sound IRR program is not built in a single ALCO meeting. Here is the practical framework for credit unions that need to upgrade their approach.

Right-Size Your Program

A $150M credit union with a straightforward balance sheet -- auto loans, first mortgages, share accounts, and CDs -- does not need the same IRR infrastructure as a $1.5B institution with mortgage-backed securities, subordinated debt, and complex derivatives. But the principles are the same: identify your exposures, measure them rigorously, monitor them frequently, and have a plan to respond.

Most credit unions in the $100M-$500M range appropriately outsource their IRR modeling to third-party vendors. That is fine. What you cannot outsource is the understanding of what those numbers mean and the discipline to act on them.

Assumptions That Make or Break Your Model

Your IRR model is a machine that converts assumptions into projections. Bad assumptions in, bad projections out. The assumptions that matter most:

  • Deposit decay rates. How fast do non-maturity deposits leave when rates rise? If your model assumes a 5% annual decay rate but your actual experience is 12%, your projected NII is materially overstated.
  • Prepayment speeds. How quickly do members refinance mortgages when rates drop? The PSA assumptions in your model need to reflect your credit union's actual prepayment experience, not national averages.
  • Non-maturity deposit behavior. Regular shares, checking accounts, and money market accounts do not have contractual maturities. How you model their rate sensitivity and duration has an outsized impact on NEV results.
  • New business assumptions. What loan production volume and deposit growth does the model assume? Overly optimistic growth assumptions can mask deteriorating risk positions.

Review and update these assumptions at least annually. Document the rationale for every change.

Stress Testing and Scenario Analysis

Beyond standard parallel rate shocks, your stress testing program should include:

  • Non-parallel yield curve shifts. Flattening, steepening, and inversion scenarios that reflect how rates actually move.
  • Basis risk scenarios. What happens if your deposit costs rise 50 basis points faster than your asset yields reprice?
  • Reverse stress testing. What rate scenario would cause your NII to decline by more than 20%? What scenario would breach your NEV limit? Working backward from the outcome tells you where your vulnerabilities are.
  • Combined scenarios. Rate shocks plus deposit outflows. Rising rates plus accelerated prepayments on adjustable-rate loans. The real world does not deliver risks one at a time.

Present stress test results to the board in terms they can act on: "Under this scenario, our annual net interest income drops by $1.2M and our net worth ratio declines from 9.8% to 8.9%." That is a conversation starter. A table full of percentages is a conversation ender.

The Daily Data Difference in IRR Management

Here is where the standard approach to interest rate risk management breaks down: the lag between when rates move and when your team sees the impact.

Most credit unions operate on a 30-to-45-day data lag. Monthly financials close sometime between the 10th and 20th of the following month. The IRR model runs after that. By the time the ALCO reviews results, the data is six to eight weeks old.

In a rate environment where the Fed can move 25 basis points in a single meeting and competitor deposit pricing can shift overnight, six-week-old data is not risk management. It is a history lesson.

Daily bottom-line visibility changes this dynamic fundamentally. When you see the spread between money bought and money sold every 24 hours, you do not need to wait for the quarterly model to tell you that basis risk is compressing your margin. You see it happening in the daily numbers and you respond in days, not months.

A $400M credit union I partnered with caught a basis risk problem through daily spread monitoring -- their deposit costs were repricing 15 basis points faster than their variable-rate assets, a pattern that was invisible in their monthly financials but showed up clearly in seven consecutive days of declining spread. They adjusted their money market pricing within 10 days. Their quarterly model would not have flagged the issue for another five weeks.

Daily data does not replace your IRR model. It complements it by giving your team current awareness of how rate movements are actually hitting your bottom line -- not how the model projected they would.

From Measurement to Action

The biggest gap in most credit union IRR programs is not measurement. It is response. Credit unions measure interest rate risk diligently. They produce reports, run scenarios, and present results to the ALCO. Then the report goes in the binder.

The gap between identifying a risk position and doing something about it is where earnings get lost. Here is how to close it.

Connect IRR Findings to Specific Decisions

Every IRR model run should produce a short list of actionable items. If your NII simulation shows that you are liability-sensitive and a +100 basis point shock would cost you $900K in annual income, the ALCO should be asking: What do we do about it?

Possible responses include adjusting loan pricing to emphasize adjustable-rate products, extending the duration of your deposit base with CD promotions, shortening your investment portfolio duration, or hedging specific exposures. Each response involves tradeoffs. The ALCO's job is to evaluate those tradeoffs and make a documented decision within two weeks -- not next quarter.

Track Execution in Tight Loops

Once the ALCO decides to act, track the execution. Did the pricing change get implemented? Did the deposit campaign launch? Did the investment portfolio actually shift? And most importantly, did the action produce the expected result?

Two-week decision cycles keep the team honest. You make a decision, execute it, measure the impact, and adjust. That is how you close the gap between IRR measurement and IRR management.

Document Everything

Every decision, every rationale, every outcome. Not because the examiner requires it (though they do), but because you cannot improve what you do not track. When the next rate cycle hits, you want a documented record of what worked, what did not, and why.

FAQ: Interest Rate Risk Management for Credit Unions

What are the four types of interest rate risk?

The four types of interest rate risk are repricing risk (timing mismatches between when assets and liabilities reset rates), basis risk (different rate indexes moving at different speeds, such as deposit costs rising faster than loan yields), yield curve risk (changes in the shape of the yield curve affecting the profitability of borrowing short and lending long), and options risk (embedded member options like mortgage prepayments, CD early withdrawals, and deposit shifts that change your balance sheet cash flows in unpredictable ways).

How do credit unions measure interest rate risk?

Credit unions use three primary measurement methods. GAP analysis measures the repricing mismatch between assets and liabilities across time buckets. Net Interest Income (NII) simulation projects how earnings would change under different rate scenarios over 12 to 24 months. Net Economic Value (NEV) calculates the present value impact on the entire balance sheet under rate shocks. NCUA expects credit unions to use both short-term (NII) and long-term (NEV) measures for a complete picture of interest rate risk exposure.

What does NCUA look for in interest rate risk management?

NCUA evaluates IRR management under the Sensitivity ("S") component of the CAMELS rating. Examiners expect to see a board-approved IRR policy with specific risk limits, an active ALCO that meets at least monthly and makes documented decisions, appropriate measurement methods (both NII and NEV), reasonable and regularly updated model assumptions, back-testing of model projections against actual results, comprehensive stress testing including non-parallel rate scenarios, and evidence that IRR findings drive actual balance sheet decisions.

What is the difference between NEV and income simulation?

Net Interest Income (NII) simulation measures the short-term earnings impact of rate changes over the next 12 to 24 months, answering the question "how much money will we make or lose if rates move?" Net Economic Value (NEV) measures the long-term impact on the entire balance sheet by calculating the present value of all assets minus all liabilities under different rate scenarios, answering "what happens to the economic value of this institution?" A credit union can show stable near-term NII while sitting on an NEV position that signals structural risk in years two through five. Both measures are necessary because they capture different dimensions of interest rate exposure.

How often should a credit union run IRR scenarios?

Most credit unions run their formal IRR model monthly using month-end data, with comprehensive stress testing at least quarterly. However, the formal model run should not be the only time your team thinks about interest rate risk. Monitoring the daily spread between money bought and money sold gives leadership continuous awareness of how rate movements are affecting the balance sheet in real time. The formal model validates the trends; daily monitoring ensures you are not surprised by them.
