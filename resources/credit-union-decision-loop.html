The 2-Week Decision Loop for Credit Unions

A 14-day decision framework that turns credit union strategy into documented action. Replace quarterly reviews with tight loops that drive accountability.

The 2-Week Decision Loop: How Credit Unions Turn Strategy Into Action

A $180M credit union discovered their auto loan portfolio was underpriced by 35 basis points. But they did not discover it in real time — they found it in the monthly board report, six weeks after the rate environment shifted. By the time they adjusted pricing, they had originated $4.2M in new auto loans at a yield that was already underwater relative to their cost of funds.

Six weeks. That is how long it took to identify a problem, discuss it, and make a decision.

This is what slow decision-making actually costs. Not in theory — in dollars. And it happens at credit unions every month because the decision cycle is built around monthly reports and quarterly board meetings instead of a tight, recurring loop connected to daily data.

The 2-week decision loop changes that. It is a structured framework for making, documenting, and reviewing strategic decisions every 14 days — fast enough to catch problems before they compound, disciplined enough to create real accountability, and documented enough to satisfy your NCUA examiner.

The Speed Problem: Why Monthly Decisions Cost You Money

Credit unions operate on a decision cadence designed for a different era. Monthly financial close. Monthly ALCO meeting. Quarterly board report. Annual strategic plan review. The fastest most leadership teams see their financial position is 30 days after the fact.

That cadence made sense when rates were stable and competitive pressure moved slowly. It does not work now.

When your cost of funds can shift 10 basis points in a month and a competitor can launch a 4.75% CD special overnight, a 30-day data lag means you are always responding to problems that have already compounded. By the time the monthly report tells you deposit runoff accelerated, you have lost $3M in core deposits. By the time the quarterly board packet shows margin compression, you have absorbed 90 days of spread erosion.

The cost is not abstract. A $250M credit union running a 2.95% net interest margin that does not catch a 15 basis point spread compression for 45 days loses roughly $46,000 in net interest income before anyone makes a single decision about it. If the decision takes another 30 days to implement, the total cost approaches $90,000 — on a single issue.

Now multiply that across three or four decisions that are all waiting in the monthly report queue. The cost of slow decisions is not a rounding error. It is the difference between a strong year and a flat one.

What a 2-Week Decision Loop Looks Like

The loop has four phases spread across 14 days. It is not a meeting marathon — it is one focused session per cycle with daily data feeding the process between sessions.

Day 1: Review and Identify

Pull up the Daily Bottom Line and Drivers and Roadblocks scorecard. Look at the last 14 days of financial data: what happened to your spread? Where did your cost of funds move? What happened to loan yield? What is the deposit mix doing?

Then review the decision log from the previous loop. Every decision made 14 days ago has an expected outcome and a review date. Did it produce the result you expected? If yes, document the win and move on. If not, diagnose why and decide whether to adjust or stay the course.

Based on the current data and previous loop outcomes, identify the top two to three decisions that need to be made in this cycle. Not ten. Not fifteen. Two or three. The discipline of prioritization is as important as the speed of the loop.

Days 2-10: Research and Prepare

Each decision gets an owner who spends the next eight days gathering what is needed: data analysis, vendor quotes, competitive intel, staff input. This is not a committee exercise — one person owns each decision and prepares a recommendation.

The daily bottom line is running the entire time. If something material changes — a large deposit walks, a delinquency spikes, a rate shift — the owner incorporates it into their recommendation. The data is never stale.

Days 11-12: Decide and Document

The leadership team meets for 60 to 90 minutes. Each decision owner presents their recommendation with the supporting data. The team discusses. A decision is made.

Every decision gets documented with five elements:

  1. What was decided — Plain language, no ambiguity. "Cap 12-month CD renewal rates at 4.00% effective April 21" not "revisit CD pricing."
  2. Who decided — The person accountable for the outcome.
  3. What data informed it — Specific metrics from the daily bottom line or scorecard.
  4. Expected outcome — A measurable result with a timeframe. "Expect $2M in CD-to-money-market migration within 30 days."
  5. Review date — When the team will check results. Default: 14 days, at the start of the next loop.

Day 14: Close the Loop

The cycle ends and the next one begins. Day 14 of this loop is Day 1 of the next. The decision log carries forward. Nothing falls through the cracks because the system itself forces follow-through.

Deciding is Doing: Why Documentation Changes Everything

Most credit unions make decisions. Few document them. Fewer still review whether those decisions worked.

The act of documentation transforms decision-making in three ways.

It creates clarity. When you have to write down exactly what was decided, ambiguity disappears. "We are going to look into that" becomes "We decided to reduce CD promotional rates by 50 basis points starting May 1, with Sarah owning the member communication plan by April 25." Everyone leaves the room knowing exactly what is happening.

It creates accountability. When a decision has a named owner, a documented rationale, and a review date, follow-through is no longer optional. The decision log is not a suggestion box — it is a commitment register. When the next loop opens, the first question is: "What happened with the decisions from the last cycle?" You cannot hide from that question when the answer is written down.

It creates an audit trail. Every documented decision — with its data, rationale, owner, and outcome — is exactly what your NCUA examiner wants to see when they evaluate management quality. The decision log is not extra work for exam prep. It is exam prep that happens as a byproduct of running your credit union well.

How Daily Data Fuels Better Decisions

You cannot run a 2-week decision loop on monthly data. The math does not work. If your data is 30 days old on Day 1 of the loop, by the time you make a decision on Day 12, you are acting on information that is 42 days stale. That is not a decision loop — that is a guessing loop.

The Daily Bottom Line changes the equation. Every morning, the leadership team sees the exact spread between money bought and money sold — updated for yesterday's activity. Not an estimate. Not a projection. The actual number.

That daily data is what makes the 2-week loop possible. When a $220M credit union started running this cadence, their CEO told me something that stuck: "For the first time, I felt like I was making decisions about what is happening, not what already happened."

The Drivers and Roadblocks scorecard adds the diagnostic layer. It does not just show you that the spread moved — it shows you why. Was it a deposit cost increase? A loan yield decline? A change in the deposit mix? A single large transaction? When you know the cause, you can make a decision about the cause — not just react to the symptom.

The Accountability Layer: No More "Who Was Supposed to Do That?"

Every credit union leader has sat in a meeting and heard this question: "I thought we decided to do that months ago. What happened?"

The answer is almost always the same: the decision was made but never documented, never assigned a clear owner, and never reviewed. It evaporated into the daily noise of running a credit union.

The 2-week decision loop eliminates this problem structurally. Accountability is not a personality trait in this system — it is a process feature. Every decision has an owner. Every owner has a deadline. Every deadline has a review date. And every review is built into the start of the next loop.

This is not micromanagement. Nobody is checking on anyone daily or demanding status updates. The system creates a natural rhythm where follow-through is the expectation because the loop demands it. When the leadership team sits down every 14 days and opens the decision log, the question is simple: "What happened?" The answer is either "here are the results" or "here is why it stalled and what we need to adjust."

That is systematic accountability. It replaces the uncomfortable interpersonal dynamics of "holding people accountable" with a process that makes accountability automatic.

What This Looks Like at a Real Credit Union

Here is a single 2-week loop at a $200M credit union, showing the framework in action.

Day 1 — Review: The leadership team opens the daily bottom line. Spread has compressed $2,100/day over the last two weeks. The Drivers and Roadblocks scorecard points to two causes: cost of funds increased 6 basis points due to $4.8M in CD renewals at 4.50%, and auto loan yield dropped 8 basis points as new originations came in at 5.85% vs. portfolio average of 6.15%. They also review the prior loop: the decision to launch a member referral program generated 23 new accounts in 14 days (target was 30). Decision: continue the program but increase the referral bonus from $50 to $75.

Day 1 — Identify: Two decisions for this loop: (1) Address CD renewal pricing — current auto-renewal at 4.50% is above market. (2) Review indirect auto loan pricing with dealer network — the 5.85% rate on new originations is below the floor needed to maintain target spread.

Days 2-10 — Prepare: The CFO pulls competitive CD rate data (market average is 4.15% for 12-month) and models the deposit retention impact of reducing the renewal rate to 4.00%. The VP of Lending contacts the three largest dealer partners to discuss pricing adjustments and pulls the last 90 days of indirect origination data by dealer.

Day 11 — Decide: (1) CD renewal rate capped at 4.00% effective immediately, with member notification letter prepared by Day 14. Owner: CFO. Expected outcome: $1.8M in renewal retention at the lower rate, $600K in attrition (acceptable). (2) Indirect auto rate floor increased from 5.75% to 6.10%. Owner: VP of Lending. Expected outcome: new origination volume decreases 15% but yield improves 25 basis points. Both decisions documented in the decision log with data, rationale, and review dates.

Day 14 — Close the loop: Both decisions are logged, owners are confirmed, and the next loop begins.

Getting Started: Your First 2-Week Loop

You do not need new software to start. You do not need a consultant. You need three things.

Daily financial data. At minimum, know your spread between money bought and money sold every day. If you do not have a system that produces this automatically, start by having your CFO calculate it manually for 14 days. It will be imperfect. That is fine. The habit matters more than the precision at this stage.

A decision log. A shared document — even a simple spreadsheet — with five columns: decision, owner, data reviewed, expected outcome, and review date. Nothing elaborate. Just a place where decisions live in writing instead of in memory.

A 60-to-90-minute block every two weeks. Put it on the calendar. Protect it. This is not an optional meeting — it is the engine that drives your strategic plan. Invite the people who make decisions: CEO, CFO, COO, and any VP who owns a strategic initiative.

Your first loop will feel uncomfortable. You will not have perfect daily data yet. Your decision log will feel awkward to fill out. Some decisions will not have clear expected outcomes because you have never been asked to define them before. That is normal. The second loop is better. By the fourth loop, it is muscle memory.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can credit unions make faster decisions?

Credit unions make faster decisions by shortening the feedback loop between data and action. Replace monthly financial reports with daily visibility into your spread between money bought and money sold. Then implement a 2-week decision loop: every 14 days, review what the data shows, identify the top decisions that need to be made, make them, document them, and close the loop. This collapses the typical decision cycle from 60-90 days to 14 days without adding meetings or bureaucracy.

What is a decision loop?

A decision loop is a recurring cycle of reviewing data, making a decision, documenting it, and then checking the results on a set schedule. In a credit union context, a 2-week decision loop means every 14 days the leadership team reviews the daily financial data, identifies the top 2-3 decisions that need to be made, documents each decision (who decided, what data informed it, what outcome is expected), and reviews the results of previous decisions. The loop creates continuous forward motion instead of the start-and-stop rhythm of quarterly reviews.

How do you document strategic decisions at a credit union?

Document every strategic decision with five elements: what was decided, who made the decision, what data informed it, what outcome is expected (with a specific metric and timeframe), and when the decision will be reviewed. Store these in a decision log that carries forward from loop to loop. This documentation creates accountability, provides an audit trail for NCUA examiners, and ensures institutional memory when leadership changes.

What is the role of the board in credit union decision making?

The board sets strategic direction, approves policy, and provides governance oversight — they do not make operational decisions. The 2-week decision loop is a management tool, not a board tool. Board members should see the decision log at regular board meetings as evidence that management is executing the strategic plan with discipline and accountability. This gives the board what they actually need: confidence that the plan is being executed, documented evidence of sound decision-making, and clear visibility into results.

How often should a credit union leadership team meet to review strategy?

Every two weeks for a focused 60-90 minute session. This is not an additional meeting — it replaces the unstructured check-ins and ad hoc conversations where most decisions currently happen. The 2-week cadence is fast enough to catch problems before they compound and slow enough to allow meaningful progress between sessions. Pair it with daily financial visibility so the leadership team has current data to act on, not month-old reports.
